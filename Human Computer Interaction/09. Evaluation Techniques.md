> [!abstract] Summary
> Evaluation is a critical component of the HCI design process. It ensures that the system not only functions as intended but is actually usable by its target audience. This note covers the transition from conceptual design evaluation to implementation testing, involving experts and real users alike.

## 1. Introduction to Evaluation

<span style="font-weight:bold;">Evaluation</span> is the process of testing the <span style="color:#e67e22;">usability</span> and <span style="color:#e67e22;">functionality</span> of a system. It is not a one-time event but a continuous activity that evaluates both the design and the implementation.

> [!info] The QA Perspective
> In traditional software engineering, most effort goes into <span style="font-weight:bold;">Functional Evaluation</span> (unit, integration, and system tests). However, <span style="color:#3498db;">Usability</span> is a "non-functional" attribute that often gets sidelined. HCI evaluation provides a dedicated approach to ensure the user experience is as robust as the backend code.

### Goals of Evaluation
The primary goals can be broken down into three pillars:
1.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Assess the extent of functionality:</span> Does the system do what it is supposed to do?
2.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Assess the effect of the interface on the user:</span> Is there a positive or negative impact on the user's workflow?
3.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Identify specific problems:</span> Pinpoint exact areas where the design fails or causes friction.

> [!help] FAQ: Definition and Goals
> **Q: What is the definition and what are the main goals of evaluation in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)?**
> **A:** Evaluation tests the usability and functionality of a system, and it evaluates both the system's design and its implementation. It can occur in a laboratory, in the field, or in collaboration with users, and should be considered throughout all stages of the design life cycle. The primary goals of evaluation are to assess the extent of the system's functionality, to assess the effect of the interface on the user, and to identify specific problems.

## 2. Evaluating Designs (Expert-Based)

Before a single line of code is written, we can evaluate <span style="color:#9b59b6;">conceptual designs</span>. These methods are typically performed by experts rather than end-users.

### A. Cognitive Walkthrough
Proposed by <span style="font-weight:bold;">Polson et al.</span>, this method focuses heavily on <span style="color:#2ecc71;">learnability</span>.

* <span style="font-weight:bold;">The Process:</span> An expert in cognitive psychology "walks through" the design like a user following a path.
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Tools:</span> They use specific forms to guide the analysis.
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Core Question:</span> Does the design lead the user to generate the <span style="font-weight:bold;">correct goals</span>?

> [!example] The Calculator Example
> Imagine a simple calculator. A cognitive walkthrough would assess the impact of inputting two numbers. Is it keys, touch, or voice? If it's a simple addition, the cognitive load is low. If it's <span style="color:#e74c3c;">matrix multiplication</span>, the cognitive requirement is much higher. The expert asks: *Can a layman learn this easily, or is it broken into small, digestible parts?*

### B. Heuristic Evaluation
Proposed by <span style="font-weight:bold;">Nielsen and Molich</span>. 

* Experts use a set of <span style="font-weight:bold;">heuristics</span> (usability criteria) to "debug" the design.
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Common Heuristics:</span>
    * System behavior is <span style="color:#3498db;">predictable</span>.
    * System behavior is <span style="color:#3498db;">consistent</span>.
    * <span style="color:#3498db;">Feedback</span> is provided for every action.

### C. Review-based & Model-based Evaluation
This uses existing <span style="font-weight:bold;">literature</span> and models to support or refute design choices.

* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Model-based:</span> Uses cognitive models like <span style="font-weight:bold;">GOMS</span> (Goals, Operators, Methods, and Selection) to predict performance.
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Design Rationale:</span> Explaining the "why" behind a design (e.g., "We put buttons on the right because users find the bottom hard to reach").

> [!caution] The Transferability Problem
> Literature from one domain might not work in another. For example, student performance trends in Computer Science might not transfer directly to Chemical Engineering. Experts must be careful when applying old research to new designs.

> [!help] FAQ: Evaluating Designs
> **Q: What methods are used for evaluating designs before they are fully implemented?**
> **A:** There are three main methods for evaluating designs:
> 1. **Cognitive Walkthrough:** Evaluates support for learning tasks using psychological principles.
> 2. **Heuristic Evaluation:** Experts check for violations of usability criteria (e.g., consistency).
> 3. **Review-based Evaluation:** Uses literature and cognitive models (GOMS) to filter design options.

## 3. Evaluating Through User Participation

When we move beyond experts, we involve the actual people who will use the system.

### Laboratory Studies
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Pros:</span> Specialist equipment, no interruptions, controlled environment.
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Cons:</span> <span style="color:#e74c3c;">Lack of context</span>. It's hard to see how people cooperate in a vacuum.

> [!example] The Bus Conductor Scenario
> You might test a ticket printing app in a quiet lab and it works perfectly. However, in a <span style="font-weight:bold;">real field study</span>, that conductor is in a crowded, moving bus, holding the device with one hand while handling cash with the other. The "lab success" might fail the "real-world" test.

### Field Studies
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Pros:</span> Natural environment, context is retained, allows for <span style="color:#2ecc71;">longitudinal studies</span> (long-term assessment).
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Cons:</span> Distractions and noise.

> [!important] Situated Action
> Field studies acknowledge that "real action is situated action." You cannot fully understand how a system works until you see it in its physical and social environment.

> [!help] FAQ: Lab vs. Field
> **Q: When evaluating through user participation, what are the differences between laboratory studies and field studies?**
> **A:** Laboratory studies use specialist equipment in a controlled, uninterrupted environment (good for dangerous locations or single-user focus) but lack natural context. Field studies take place in natural environments, retaining context and enabling longitudinal studies, though they are prone to distractions and noise.

## 4. Experimental Evaluation (Implementation)

To evaluate an implementation, you need an <span style="font-weight:bold;">artifact</span> (simulation, prototype, or full system).

### Experimental Factors
1.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Subjects:</span> Who are they? They must be a <span style="color:#3498db;">representative sample</span>.
2.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Variables:</span> 
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Independent (IV):</span> What you change (e.g., font size, interface style).
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Dependent (DV):</span> What you measure (e.g., time taken, error rate).
3.  <span style="font-weight:bold;">Hypothesis:</span> A prediction of the relationship between IV and DV.

> [!tip] The "Honey vs. Medicine" Example
> To test a new COVID medicine, you give one group the medicine and another a "placebo" (honey) that looks/tastes the same. In HCI, you might give Group A a "Menu" and Group B "Icons" to see which is faster.

### Statistical Hypotheses
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Null Hypothesis (H0):</span> States there is <span style="font-weight:bold;">no difference</span> between conditions (e.g., "Changing font size doesn't change error rate").
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Alternative Hypothesis (Ha):</span> States there is a difference. The goal is to disprove the Null.
### Experimental Design
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Within Groups:</span> Each user tries <span style="font-weight:bold;">every</span> condition. (Cheaper, but users might "learn" the task and get better naturally).
* <span style="font-weight:bold;">Between Groups:</span> Each user tries only <span style="font-weight:bold;">one</span> condition. (No learning transfer, but requires more people and variation can bias results).

## 5. Data Analysis

> [!warning] Sanity Check
> 
> Before running complex stats, look at your data and save the original copies. Ensure it makes common sense first!

- Discrete Data: Finite values (e.g., number of errors).
    
- Continuous Data: Any value within a range (e.g., time in seconds).
    
### Types of Tests

- Parametric: Assumes a normal distribution. Powerful and robust.
    
- Non-parametric: Does not assume normal distribution. More reliable when data is messy or skewed.
    
- Significance / contingency ($p$-value): We usually look for $p < 0.05$. This means there is a 95% confidence that the difference observed is real and not just a fluke of the data.

## 6. Group Evaluation

Evaluating groups is significantly harder than evaluating a single user.

> [!fail] The Group Challenge
> 
> Groups are expensive, take longer to "settle down," and are a nightmare to timetable. This is why researchers often settle for only 3 or 4 groups.

- Tasks: Must encourage cooperation.
    
    - Creative: "Write a report together."
        
    - Decision: "Desert Survival Task" (deciding what items to keep).
        
    - Control: "ARKola bottling plant" simulation.
        
- Data: You need multiple cameras and direct logging. The volume of data and synchronization issues are massive.

## 7. Observational Methods

### Think Aloud

The user performs a task while describing what they are doing and why.

- Advantage: Simple, reveals the user's mental model.
    
- Disadvantage: Describing the task can actually change how they perform it (interference).
    
### Cooperative Evaluation

A variation where the user and evaluator collaborate.

- The evaluator can ask, "Why did you click that?" and the user can ask, "What does this icon mean?"
    
- It breaks the "silent observer" barrier and encourages the user to criticize the system.
    
### Post-task Walkthroughs

Recording the session and playing it back to the user for comment.

- Immediate: Actions are fresh in the mind.
    
- Delayed: Evaluator has time to identify specific confusing moments to ask about.
    
- Risk: Users might "invent" a reason for a mistake they made to avoid looking silly (post-hoc interpretation).

## 8. Query Techniques

### Interviews

- One-on-one sessions.
    
- Can be Open (free-flowing), Closed (strict questions), or Semi-structured.
    
- Rich, high-quality data but extremely time-consuming.
    

### Questionnaires

- Fixed questions for large groups.
    
- Styles: General, Open-ended, Multi-choice, or Scalar (e.g., Likert scale 1-5).
    

> [!attention] Avoid Leading Questions
> 
> Questions like "HCI is an important subject, don't you agree?" are skewed. A balanced questionnaire shouldn't push the user toward a specific answer.

## 9. Physiological Methods

These measure physical responses to understand emotional states.

### Eye Tracking

Tracks the gaze using head or desk-mounted cameras.

- Fixations: Where the eye stops. Indicates difficulty or interest.
    
- Saccades: Rapid jumps between points.
    
- Scan Paths: The route the eye takes.
    

> [!quote] The $3 Million Device
> 
> High-precision eye trackers used in psychological research can cost millions of dollars to achieve "pixel-level" accuracy, though modern webcams can now do basic tracking for cheap.

### Biosensors

- GSR (Galvanic Skin Response): Sweat gland activity (stress).
    
- EMG/EEG: Muscle and brain electrical activity.
    
- Heart Rate: Blood pressure and pulse.

## 10. Choosing an Evaluation Method

> [!done] Checklist for Selection
> 
> When picking a method, consider these factors:
> 
> - **Stage:** Are you evaluating a Design or an Implementation?
>     
> - **Style:** Lab vs. Field.
>     
> - **Objectivity:** Subjective vs. Objective.
>     
> - **Measure:** Qualitative (words/observations) vs. Quantitative (numbers/stats).
>     
> - **Interference:** Obtrusive (obvious monitoring) vs. Unobtrusive (silent logging).
>     
> - **Resources:** Time, subjects, equipment, and expertise available.
>     

> [!important] Final Note on Ethics
> 
> Always use a Consent Form. Clearly state: "This experiment evaluates the system, not you." If users feel like they are being tested on their intelligence, their stress levels will spike and ruin your data!

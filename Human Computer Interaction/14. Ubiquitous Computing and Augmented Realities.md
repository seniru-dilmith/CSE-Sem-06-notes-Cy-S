> [!abstract] <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#e74c3c;">Overview & Core Distinction</span>
> The realm of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is rapidly evolving beyond the traditional desktop workstation. This lecture covers two broad, emerging technological paradigms: <span style="color:#2980b9; font-weight:bold;">Ubiquitous Computing</span> and <span style="color:#8e44ad; font-weight:bold;">Virtual/Augmented Realities</span>. 
> 
> <span style="background-color:#f1c40f; color:#2c3e50; font-weight:bold;">The Fundamental Difference:</span>
> Ubiquitous computing focuses on "filling the real world with computers" (automation and integration into everyday environments), whereas virtual and augmented reality focus on "making the real world in a computer" or intermingling the physical and digital environments to simulate real-like experiences.

> [!quote] <span style="font-weight:bold;">Challenging HCI Assumptions</span>
> <span style="font-style:italic; color:#7f8c8d;">"The most profound technologies are those that disappear."</span> - Mark Weiser (1990s)
> This foundational quote challenges our imagination of what a computer is. It implies that technology should seamlessly blend into our background activities, moving away from explicit workstations to invisible, continuous assistance.

## <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#2980b9;">1. Ubiquitous Computing (Ubicomp)</span>

> [!question] <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#d35400;">What is Ubiquitous Computing (Ubicomp) and what scales of devices did it introduce?</span>
> Ubicomp refers to any computing technology that permits human interaction away from a single, traditional workstation. It fulfills Mark Weiser's vision of technology disappearing into the background. To categorize this, Weiser proposed three specific device scales based on size and the relationship they have with people: <span style="font-weight:bold;">Inch, Foot, and Yard</span>.

### <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#27ae60;">Scales of Devices</span>

The scale of devices drastically changes how humans interact with them, the level of ownership, and their typical use cases.

![[Pasted image 20260523195652.png]]

* <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b;">Inch Scale:</span>
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Examples:</span> Smartphones, Voice Recorders, PDAs (Personal Digital Assistants).
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Characteristics:</span> Individuals typically own many of them. They are small, personal, and capable of communicating with each other and the surrounding environment. Modern smartphones have essentially replaced older PDA technologies.
* <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b;">Foot Scale:</span>
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Examples:</span> Notebooks, Tablets, Digital Paper.
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Characteristics:</span> Individuals own several of these but are not assumed to carry them at all times. They provide a larger canvas for work but remain somewhat portable.
* <span style="font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b;">Yard Scale:</span>
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Examples:</span> Electronic whiteboards, Plasma displays, Smart bulletin boards.
    * <span style="font-weight:bold;">Characteristics:</span> These are bulky, large-scale displays. Individuals do not own them; buildings or institutions own them, and lots of people share and interact with them simultaneously.

### Defining the Interaction Experience

> [!question] How does ubiquitous computing change input and output interaction?
> 
> It shifts the paradigm from traditional explicit input (like typing on a keyboard) to implicit, sensor-based input. It also expands output from single screens to multi-scale, distributed, ambient, and peripheral displays.

#### Input Modalities

Ubicomp extends traditional explicit inputs (keyboard, mouse) toward system "awareness."

- Implicit Input: Uses sensor-based inputs such as proximity sensors, infrared receivers, capacitive touch on tables, and tilt sensors (2-axis linear accelerometers).
    
- Recognition Technologies: Incorporates speech, light, and visual recognition.
    
- > [!warning] Ambiguity:
    > 
    > Because recognizers (sensors, speech-to-text) are never 100% perfect, this introduces a level of ambiguity and potential error that system designers must account for.
    

#### Output Experiences

Outputs are no longer just eye-grabbing raster displays. They are multi-scale and distributed in space, yet coordinated.

- Ambient Output: Uses features of the physical environment to signal information (e.g., The Water Lamp).
    
- Peripheral Output: Designed to remain in the background without demanding direct attention (e.g., The Dangling String).
    

### Application Themes and Theories

> [!info] Merging Physical and Digital Worlds
> 
> The goal is to remove the barrier between physical and digital spaces. Actions on a physical object should have electronic meaning, and vice versa. An example is a "digital desk" or an augmented calendar (e.g., NaviCam) where electronic output is superimposed on the physical world.

- Context-aware computing: Sensed phenomena facilitate easier, automated interactions.
    
- Automated capture and access: Live experiences are stored for future access (removing the need for repetitive manual input).
    
- Continuous interaction: Everyday activities do not have clear begin-end conditions.
    

> [!note] New Opportunities for Theory
> 
> HCI theories are adapting to this new landscape:
> 
> 1. Knowledge in the world & Activity Theory: Emphasizes the physical world, where goals and actions fluidly adjust to the physical state.
>     
> 2. Situated action & Distributed cognition: Focuses on improvisational and opportunistic behavior rather than strictly planned actions.
>     
> 3. Ethnography: Requires deep, descriptive understanding of activities strictly within their natural context.
>     

> [!failure] Evaluation Challenges
> 
> Standard HCI evaluation techniques are incredibly difficult to adapt here.
> 
> - Ubicomp activities are not task-centric.
>     
> - Getting long-term authentic summative evaluation is hard due to the novelty of the technology.
>     
> - Metrics of success differ: Instead of pure efficiency, metrics might measure "playfulness" or "non-distraction."
>     

### Ubicomp Examples

> [!example] Example 1: Ambient Wood
> 
> A real woodland environment filled with hidden electronics. It features light and moisture meters that record data with GPRS locations to be drawn on a map later. It uses an RFID "periscope" to show invisible things and utilizes triggered ambient sounds based on user location.

> [!example] Example 2: City - Shared Experience (Mackintosh Interpretation Centre)
> 
> Visitors can experience the center in different ways: some on the web, some using VR, and some physically there. They can interact via microphones and 'see' each other virtually. This showcases a shared experience across different places and modalities (e.g., Zoom meetings serve as a common modern equivalent where hundreds interact effortlessly across different locations).

## 2. Virtual Reality (VR)

> [!info] VR - Technology & Experience
> 
> VR aims to simulate the real world completely inside a computer. The primary goal is immersion, engagement, and interaction.

> [!question] What hardware technologies are utilized in Virtual Reality?
> 
> VR aims to simulate the real world visually, haptically, and auditorily. Technologies include:
> 
> - VR Headsets: Provide a small TV screen for each eye at slightly different angles to create a deep 3D effect.
>     
> - DataGlove: A lycra glove equipped with optical sensors that measure exact hand and finger positions to achieve precise gesture recognition.
>     
> - Eyegaze & Body Tracking: Allows users to indicate direction with eyes alone, and senses whole-body positions like walking.
>     

> [!question] What are the primary aims and applications of VR?
> 
> The main goal is immersion. It allows users to experience situations that are too dangerous or too expensive to replicate in reality, or to see "unseen things" (too small, too large, or hidden, such as manipulating molecules).

Core Applications of VR:

- Simulation: Used heavily in gaming, military training, and flight/ship simulators.
    
- VR Holidays: Experiencing a rainforest, safari, surfing, skiing, or even a moon walk from an armchair.
    
- Medical:
    
    - Surgery: Scans and X-rays build a model, allowing surgeons to "practice" an operation, especially when enhanced with force feedback (haptics).
        
    - Phobia Treatment: Exposure therapy using virtual lifts, spiders, etc., to help minimize fears safely.
        

> [!question] How does Desktop VR differ from Headset VR?
> 
> While Headset VR provides incredible immersion, it can be expensive, heavy, and uncomfortable for prolonged use.
> 
> Desktop VR uses an ordinary monitor and PC. It is cheap, convenient, and widely used in gaming and on the web (often utilizing VRML - Virtual Reality Markup Language for rendering 3D environments).

> [!tip] Command and Control Centers
> ![[Pasted image 20260523200505.png]]
> Specialized VR setups use scenes projected on walls to create highly realistic environments. They feature hydraulic rams, real physical controls, and allow multiple people to collaborate simultaneously (e.g., high-end flight simulators).

## 3. Augmented Reality (AR)

> [!info] Mixing Virtual and Real
> 
> Augmented Reality (AR) projects digital images and information over the real world. Examples include aircraft head-up displays, semi-transparent goggles, and desktop projections.

> [!question] What is Augmented Reality (AR) and what are its major challenges?
> 
> AR overlays virtual data onto physical environments. The information can be unrelated (like reading an email on a wearable glass) or related (virtual objects actively interacting with the physical world).
> 
> The major challenges include eye gaze direction and registration - the highly difficult process of aligning virtual constructs perfectly with real-world physical objects.

> [!question] How is AR used practically for maintenance?
> 
> AR is heavily used to overlay maintenance instructions and display complex schematics directly onto hardware.
> 
> - Photocopier Engineers: Use AR where registration is critical. Virtual arrows must point exactly to the correct physical parts to guide repairs.
>     
> - Aircraft Wiring Looms: Here, precise registration might be too difficult due to the complexity of the wires. Instead, general schematics are displayed to aid the engineer without mapping 1:1 to every wire.
>     

## 4. Information and Data Visualisation

> [!question] How are these technologies used for Information and Data Visualisation?
> 
> VR, AR, and 3D displays are leveraged to make scientific and complex data understandable. Interactivity is central to this. Data can be mapped into multiple spaces.

### Visualising Scientific and Technical Data

1. Three Dimensional Space (3D): Visualizes invisible fields or values.
    
    - > [!example] Virtual Wind Tunnel
        > ![[Pasted image 20260523200753.png]]
        > Uses fluid dynamics to simulate air flow with virtual bubbles to show movements. It is considered "better" than a physical wind tunnel because there is no physical disruption of air flow (no physical probes or malfunctioning fans), and it is significantly cheaper and faster to run simulations.
        
        2. Two Dimensional Space (2D): Projects data values up from a plane (e.g., geographic data). A challenge here is that viewing angles can be hard for static visualizations.
            
2. No 'Real' Dimensions: Translating abstract numbers into 2D/3D histograms, scatter plots, and pie charts.
    

### Structured Information

> [!question] How is structured information (like hierarchies and networks) visualized?
> 
> Information systems have complex data types unlike pure scientific numbers.

- Hierarchies (e.g., file trees, organization charts):
    
    - Traditional 2D organizational charts break down and become impossible to read when they get too wide.
        ![[Pasted image 20260523200937.png]]
    - > [!success] Solution: 3D Cone Trees (Xerox)
        > ![[Pasted image 20260523200903.png]]
        > By moving to 3D, levels become rings. Overlap is acceptable and navigable in 3D space, easily solving the "wide hierarchy" problem.
        
- Networks (e.g., program flow charts, hypertext structures):
    
    - Networks are viewed as "graphs" containing nodes (like web pages) and links (directed or undirected).
        
    - Planar graphs can be drawn without crossing lines.
        
    - Non-planar graphs will inevitably have crossing lines in any 2D layout, requiring 3D representation for clear visualization.

### Time and Interactivity

> [!question] How is time incorporated into these visualisations?
> 
> Time adds a critical, interactive dimension.
> 
> - Visualising IN time: The time dimension is mapped to physical space. Examples include sales graphs changing over time, Gantt charts, timelines, or historical charts (e.g., Lifelines for visualizing medical and court records).
>     
> - USING time: The data dimension is mapped to time. Examples include the fast/slow replay of events, or mapping space to time (e.g., the Visible Human Project).
>     
> - Interactivity: Fundamental to this process, where changes happen directly under user control (e.g., influence explorer).
>     

## 5. Innovative Developments & Future Trends (Deep-Dive)

> [!todo] Lecture Video Additional Insights
> 
> The transcript emphasizes that while Ubicomp, VR, and AR are established concepts, the hardware implementing them is continuously evolving. A 2017 research paper on HCI trends was highlighted to showcase the trajectory of these technologies.

### Emerging Specialized Input/Output

- Curved & Ultra-High Definition (UHD/4K) flexible displays: Enhancing the visual output scale.
    
- Virtual Keyboards: Keys projected onto physical tables, allowing users to type without carrying bulky hardware (highly relevant for mobile/smartphones).
    
- Voice & Hand Gesture Recognition: Constant ongoing research. The transcript highlights Voice Biometrics (speaker recognition/verification), which uses voice to identify individuals despite the high error rates that natural language processing models currently face.
    
- Brain Signal Capturing Headsets: Used for commercial data collection and advanced experimentation.
    
### Wearables & Smart Technologies

- AR/VR Hardware: Microsoft HoloLens, Oculus Rift, HTC Vive, and accessible forms like Google Cardboard.
    
- Fashion & Retail: Virtual mannequins and virtual fitting rooms.
    
- Micro-Wearables: Smart glasses, smartwatches, smart jewelry (rings, necklaces acting as HCI interfaces), and smart footwear with embedded sensing devices.
    
- Health & Sports: Fitness tracking devices (Fitbits), M-Tracer, Corpse analyzers measuring speed and movement.
    
- Biomedical: Smart contact lenses, bionic contact lenses, and advanced ultrasound fingerprint sensors.
    
### Automotive & Environment Automation

- Autonomous Driving: Systems like Tesla's auto-pilot.
    
- Automobile Projection: Head-up displays for safety, heart attack predictor devices embedded in car seats.
    
- Smart Home & Misc: IoT integration with smart thermostats, smart plugs, baby monitors providing real-time data, and real-time natural language translation tools to decode foreign signs instantaneously to prevent hazardous situations.
    

> [!check] Conclusion: Between Two Worlds
> 
> We stand between computers filling the real world (Ubiquitous Computing) and the real world being represented inside the computer (VR/Visualisation). As AR and ambient displays mature, the physical and digital worlds will seamlessly intermingle, rendering the underlying technology virtually invisible.

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">The Core Philosophy of HCI Learning</span>

The course is structured around primary learning objectives and core topic areas that map to how we approach HCI in the real world. 

> [!info] The Golden Rule: Read a Book!
> While lectures and slides provide the framework, the depth of your understanding will come from reading the prescribed textbooks and literature. Relying solely on summarized notes is insufficient for mastering the nuances of human-computer interaction.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">1. Facts (Understanding the Real World)</span>
Facts give us the reality of the world and the limitations of both systems and humans. 
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Human Facts:</span> We design for humans who typically have two eyes, two ears, and ten fingers. While exceptions exist, general interactions (like a standard keyboard) are built on these biological facts.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">System Facts:</span> Systems rely on an Input-Process-Output (IPO) model. There are unchangeable realities like network latencies, quality of service (QoS) limits, and throughput constraints. For instance, achieving 100% security or 100% availability is practically impossible. 

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">2. Analysis</span>
Analysis involves developing a deep understanding of the issues that arise when humans interact with computers. Instead of just looking at raw data (like a raw image), analysis allows us to interpret it and understand the underlying behaviors and limitations.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">3. Design</span>
Design is where engineering comes into play. It is the process of moving from an understanding of facts and analysis to providing actual solutions. 
> [!tip] The Shift in Industry Focus
> Decades ago, backend systems (OS, databases, complex algorithms) were the primary challenge. Today, these are often readily available as frameworks and libraries. The modern software industry places massive prominence on the interface and user interaction. The <span style="font-weight:bold;">unique value proposition</span> of a product now heavily relies on how attractive and interactive it is, sometimes even at the cost of sub-optimal backend algorithms.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">4. Attitude</span>
This refers to thinking about real use and real users. 
> [!example] Memory Optimization vs. User Experience
> In the past, writing programs required heavy focus on memory allocation. Saving 1KB of memory was a significant achievement. Today, developers often prioritize rapid delivery and user experience over micro-optimizing memory, because users will not wait for a perfectly optimized system if the interface is frustrating. The right attitude in HCI is prioritizing what the user actually experiences over strict technical perfection.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">5. Design Basics (The Process of Creation)</span>
To create effective interfaces, we must master the core processes of HCI design. This topic area covers the entire lifecycle of a product's interface.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Process:</span> Understanding the iterative cycles of ideation, user-centered design, and journey mapping.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Evaluation:</span> Learning how to test designs with real users through heuristic evaluations, A/B testing, and direct feedback.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Experience:</span> Ensuring the final product not only works functionally but provides a seamless, engaging, and meaningful user experience.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">6. Underlying Knowledge and Theory</span>
Great design is not just visual; it is deeply rooted in science and human behavior. This area bridges computer science with psychology.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Human Perception:</span> How users visually and audibly process information on a screen, including color theory and visual hierarchy.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Cognition:</span> Understanding mental models, memory load, attention spans, and how users learn to navigate unfamiliar systems.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Emotion:</span> Designing interfaces that evoke trust and satisfaction, while mitigating user frustration and anxiety.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">7. Implementation (Architecture & Prototypes)</span>
Theoretical design must eventually be built. This topic area focuses on the technical execution of HCI concepts.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Architecture:</span> Structuring the frontend and backend systems to support rapid, fluid user interactions without disruptive lag.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Prototypes:</span> Moving from low-fidelity paper sketches or wireframes to high-fidelity, interactive digital models to test viability before full-scale coding begins.

### <span style="color:darkblue; font-weight:bold;">8. Specific Topics and Domains</span>
As technology evolves, HCI expands into specialized fields that require unique interaction paradigms.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Information Visualisation:</span> Transforming massive, complex datasets into understandable, interactive graphical representations for human consumption.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Physicality:</span> Exploring interaction beyond the flat screen. This includes tangible interfaces, wearable computing, smart environments, and how physical movement bridges the gap between humans and digital systems.

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">What is HCI?</span>

After about 40 years of evolution, computer science now recognizes HCI as one of its core pillars. While the industry often uses the modern term <span style="color:purple; font-weight:bold;">UX (User Experience)</span>, HCI remains the correct theoretical term. 

HCI operates on two distinct frontiers:

> [!abstract] 1. An Academic Discipline
> This involves studying people interacting with computer technology. It seeks to answer "why" questions. For example, why do users act lazy? Why do they prefer a certain interface even if it is technically less efficient? It builds the theories that explain user behavior.

> [!abstract] 2. A Design Discipline
> This is the engineering side - designing interventions for systems involving people and technology. It takes the theories from the academic side and implements them as solutions (modeling, prototyping, evaluation) for the general public.

### <span style="font-weight:bold;">The Roots and Branches of HCI</span>
HCI is unique because it is assembled from theoretical pieces of various domains.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Roots (Where it comes from):</span> Psychology, Computing, Sociology, Business & Management.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Branches (What it has grown into):</span> Visualisation, CSCW (Computer Supported Cooperative Work), Ubiquitous Computing, E-learning.

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">The Three Core "Use" Words in Design</span>

When evaluating a design in HCI, it must satisfy three criteria to be successful:

1. <span style="color:green; font-weight:bold;">Useful:</span> It must be functional. A calculator must be able to add and multiply. If it lacks required functionality, it is useless.
2. <span style="color:green; font-weight:bold;">Usable:</span> It must be easy to use and do the right things without leading the user into dangerous errors. It should provide an enjoyable user experience.
3. <span style="color:green; font-weight:bold;">Used:</span> It must be attractive, available, and acceptable. 
> [!failure] Example of a system that was Useful and Usable, but not Used
> Google Wave and Google Circles had funding, resources, and functionality. However, they failed to attract a critical mass of users. Without being <span style="font-style:italic;">used</span>, the products could not be sustained and were abandoned.

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">Changes and Trends: Increasing Multiplicity</span>

The way humans interact with computers has drastically shifted over the decades:

- <span style="color:darkred; font-weight:bold;">1980s:</span> Personal computers. The paradigm was <span style="font-style:italic;">"one man and his machine"</span>. Computing was niche, expensive, and mostly reserved for experts or a "nerdy/geek" demographic.
- <span style="color:darkred; font-weight:bold;">Late 1980s & 1990s:</span> The internet and hypertext web arrived. Computers became cheaper and accessible to lots of people. Users were geographically remote, but the paradigm remained <span style="font-style:italic;">one person per machine and one machine per person</span>.
- <span style="color:darkred; font-weight:bold;">NOW:</span> Domestic use, global networks, ubiquitous devices. We connect families and friends together and remotely. The paradigm is broken - one person uses multiple machines daily (smartphones, laptops, smart TVs), and machines talk to other machines without human intervention.

> [!quote] The Evolution of Interaction Time
> In the 80s, a user might interact with a computer for a few minutes. In the 90s, maybe 30 minutes. In the 2000s, 1-2 hours. Today, from waking up to going to sleep, users interact with computing devices for 5-8+ hours. Because it is a "partner in life," the convenience of the interaction is more critical than ever.

---

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">Ubiquitous & Wearable Computing</span>

Today, sensors and devices are everywhere. We no longer just sit at a desk; we have computers in our pockets, on our wrists, and in our home appliances.

### <span style="font-weight:bold;">From Dialogue with the Computer to Dialogue with the World</span>
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Past:</span> The computer was a dedicated translator. A human inputted commands, the computer translated them.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Present:</span> The computer is merely an interface to communicate with the world. When you use a smartphone to text a friend, your focus is on the friend, not the device. If your laptop breaks, you seamlessly switch to your phone to continue the conversation.
- <span style="font-weight:bold;">Future (Ubicomp & Cyborgs):</span> Eventually, there will be <span style="font-weight:bold;">no computer/artifact divide</span> (the physical object is the computer) and <span style="font-weight:bold;">no computer/user divide</span> (wearables and implants). Interaction will become entirely implicit.

> [!hint] The Offloading of Cognition
> Modern systems glue processes together. If you want to share a contact, the system predicts the last person you called. Spell checkers correct our words automatically. We are slowly turning off parts of our brain's active memory tasks and offloading them to computer-supported interaction.

---

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">Work vs. Fun: Empowering the User</span>

Traditional HCI methods were strictly about tasks, goals, and work. The computer dictated the workflow. If you typed the wrong thing, it said "Command not found," forcing you to adapt to the machine.

Now, HCI involves internet shopping, communities, home life, and enjoyment. 
> [!success] The Importance of Decision Points
> Modern interfaces give users more decision points. If an app doesn't work, the user simply closes it and tries another. Users feel valued, achieved, and empowered when they are in control, rather than following rigid computer instructions. Freedom and flexibility equal a positive user experience.

# <span style="font-weight:bold;">Q&A Review Section</span>

> [!question] What are the primary learning objectives of the course?
> Students are expected to learn facts about systems and humans, develop analysis skills for a deep understanding of issues, learn design to turn understanding into solutions, and cultivate an attitude of thinking about real use and real users.

> [!question] What specific topic areas does the course cover?
> The course is divided into four main areas: design basics (process, evaluation, and experience), underlying knowledge and theory (such as human perception, cognition, and emotion), implementation (architecture and prototypes), and specific topics/domains (like information visualization and physicality).

> [!question] How is the module structured in terms of learning activities and assessments?
> The Teaching and Learning Activities (TLAs) consist of lectures, discussions, and self-study. The assessment tasks are split between Continuous Assessment (CA) at 40% (which includes assignments and group work) and a Final Examination at 60% (a closed-book written paper).

> [!question] What are the self-study requirements for the students?
> Students are required to read the textbook, <span style="font-style:italic;">Human-Computer Interaction (3rd Ed)</span> by Alan Dix, Janet Finlay, Gregory D. Abowd, and Russell Beale. Additionally, self-study includes reading textbook chapters, exploratory learning with other resources, and Outcome Based Education activities.

> [!question] How is Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) defined?
> HCI is defined as both an academic discipline (studying people interacting with computer technology) and a design discipline (designing interventions for systems involving people and technology).

> [!question] What fields form the roots of HCI, and what branches have grown from it?
> HCI's many roots include psychology, computing, sociology, and business & management. Its branches have expanded to include fields like CSCW (computer supported cooperative work), visualization, ubiquitous computing, and e-learning.

> [!question] What are the three core "use" words in HCI design?
> Design in HCI focuses on making systems:
> 1. <span style="font-weight:bold;">Useful:</span> Functional and capable of doing things.
> 2. <span style="font-weight:bold;">Usable:</span> Easy to use, doing the right things, and providing an enjoyable user experience.
> 3. <span style="font-weight:bold;">Used:</span> Attractive, available, and acceptable to the organization.

> [!question] How has the "multiplicity" of computer usage changed from the 1980s to now?
> In the 1980s, computing was characterized by personal computers - "one man and his machine". By the late 1980s and 1990s, networks connected geographically remote users, but the paradigm remained one person per machine and one machine per person. NOW, computing involves domestic use, global networks, and ubiquitous devices connecting lots of people together and remotely (like families and friends).

> [!question] What characterizes ubiquitous and wearable computing today?
> It is characterized by having sensors and devices everywhere, raising questions about how many computers individuals have in their houses or even in their pockets.

> [!question] How has the human-computer relationship shifted due to these trends?
> We have moved from having a direct "dialogue with the computer" to a "dialogue with the world". In ubiquitous computing, there is no longer a divide between the computer and the artifact, and in wearable/cyborg computing, there is no divide between the computer and the user.

> [!question] How have traditional HCI methods evolved regarding "work and fun"?
> Traditional HCI methods heavily focused on tasks, goals, and work, with only the occasional game. Modern HCI encompasses much more, including internet shopping, communities, home life, overall experience, and enjoyment, leading to more decision points in the design process.

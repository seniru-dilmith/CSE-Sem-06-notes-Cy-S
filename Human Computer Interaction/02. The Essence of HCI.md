## 1. Introduction: Who is the User?

If you delve deep into the idea of Human-Computer Interaction, the first question that arises is: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Who actually uses our computers?</span> The concept of users is everywhere, and even a remote person who might not interact directly with a system can still be defined as a user.

> [!faq] Q: Why do we care about Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)?
> A: We care about HCI because users are everywhere.

To understand the scale and specialty of different users, consider the following hierarchy of user interaction:

* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Developing a new API for a programming library:</span> The users are the specific developers who use that library. The scale might be small (e.g., 10 to 50 users), but they require highly specific knowledge.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Constructing a new programming language:</span> The user base grows compared to a single library. You no longer expect extreme specialty in one niche; users will utilize the language for general tasks and processing.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Creating a website:</span> The user is anyone with internet access. Specialty requirements drop significantly, but the scale becomes massive.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Proving P = NP:</span> This affects the entire world, the whole computing industry, and core computing models. Direct users (those who read the proof) might be few, but the indirect impact is global.

> [!faq] Q: Who is considered a "user" in the context of HCI?
> A: A user can be anyone interacting with a system, including developers using a new API for a programming library, programmers using a new programming language, visitors to a website, or even someone attempting to prove mathematical concepts like P = NP.

> [!example] The Political Analogy
> Understanding your users is similar to politics. Politicians understand their users (voters) very well. Out of a population, they cater to the satisfaction of the voting majority to get elected. Similarly, anything you deliver or service you provide requires defining your users and their traits first.

> [!tip] Additional Note: Stakeholders vs. End-Users
> It helps to draw a line between direct and indirect users. In software engineering, we often map these out as stakeholders. Even if someone never touches the keyboard, if the system's output affects their daily workflow or data security, they are a critical part of the user ecosystem that HCI must account for.

## 2. The Core Essence of HCI

If you look at software engineering, it is about how humans interact with software. If you look at information systems, it is about solving end-user problems. But in computer science, HCI is highly abstract. 

> [!quote] The Crux of the Matter
> One key area of skill development in HCI is asking the <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">right questions</span> with respect to the usage of our systems, and answering them convincingly. The crux of the matter is that it is often very difficult to identify the right questions to ask.

> [!faq] Q: What is the "essence" and the "crux" of HCI?
> A: The essence of HCI is the ability to ask the right questions and answer them convincingly. The crux of the matter is that it is often very difficult to identify what those right questions are.

### The Relativity of "Right" Answers
Answering questions "convincingly" does not necessarily mean finding the absolute right answer - it means finding the right answer <span style="font-weight: bold; background-color: #fff3cd;">for a given context</span>.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Contextual History:</span> Nobody uses a typewriter today, nor do they want brick-sized cell phones. But when they were popular, they were the <span style="font-style: italic;">right answers</span> to the questions of that era.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Subjectivity:</span> HCI is subjective. People's preferences, cultural factors, and needs differ. You might design a technically poor website that still has high usability regard from users, while technically perfect systems fail.

> [!warning] Optimization vs. Usability
> In computer science, we often optimize technical parameters (memory consumption, CPU footprint, schema design). However, in general-purpose information systems, optimization is irrelevant if the users are not happy. A project manager optimizes for delivery time, cost, and maximum user satisfaction - not just CPU usage. If you do not ask the critical question ("Will the users be happy to use this?"), your product will fail regardless of technical perfection.

> [!note] Additional Note: The Intersection of HCI and Machine Learning
> When building machine learning models, developers often optimize heavily for raw accuracy, minimizing loss, or improving F1 scores using frameworks like TensorFlow. However, from an HCI perspective, the "right question" shifts. A model that is 99% accurate but acts like a "black box" might be rejected by users who need explainability and trust. True optimization in a user-facing AI system balances statistical accuracy with transparent, user-friendly feedback loops.

> [!example] The WhatsApp / Facebook Phenomenon
> Many successful products like WhatsApp, Facebook, or YouTube are conceptually similar to second-year undergraduate projects. We used to write chat engines, but they never reached millions of users. Why do some apps succeed while hundreds of thousands of similar apps fail? They asked the right questions regarding user needs. 
> Similarly, a fast-food outlet in a remote area serves the same food that a 5-star hotel serves at 20 times the price. The difference lies in branding, appearance, and asking the right questions to target user psychology.

### Impact and Strategy

> [!faq] Q: Why is mastering the essence of HCI important?
> A: If you can master asking and answering the right questions, you can have a tremendous impact on society and truly make a difference. Furthermore, these skills are tremendously valuable for successful careers in academia and industry, as well as for personal intellectual development.

> [!faq] Q: What is the strategy and expected outcome of learning about HCI?
> A: The strategy involves a mix of theory and practice, interleaving expositions of user interface techniques with discussions of evaluation methods. The outcomes include learning how to ask research questions, set up hypotheses, and accept or reject them based on evidence. Students will also learn how to think critically about user interfaces, products, and brands.

> [!tip] The Importance of Applied Statistics
> Because human behavior is noisy and subjective, you cannot simply say a task that takes 21 seconds is fundamentally worse than one taking 20 seconds. You must use applied statistics to set up a <span style="font-weight: bold;">hypothesis</span>, conduct controlled experiments, and perform significance tests to eliminate Type 1 and Type 2 errors. This is true engineering: understanding constraints, exploring parameters, and statistically validating the best solution rather than blindly using abstracted frameworks.

## 3. The Paradigm Shift: Ubiquitous Computing

We are currently transitioning from traditional desktop setups to environments where computing is everywhere.

> [!faq] Q: What major computing shift are we currently witnessing?
> A: We are on the verge of witnessing a large-scale move towards truly ubiquitous and pervasive computing systems.

> [!faq] Q: What types of pervasive computing devices are rapidly becoming commodities?
> A: Wall-sized displays, full-body motion sensors, microphone arrays, and pervasive eye-tracking systems are all rapidly becoming commodity devices.

Ubiquitous computing means the environment senses you, updates records, and delivers services without you even noticing you are interacting with a computer. 

> [!faq] Q: What are the benefits of these ubiquitous computing devices?
> A: They enable the design of non-intrusive, natural user interfaces that allow users to perform advanced tasks using only speech and gestures. Importantly, they also allow for the creation of smart, inclusive interfaces that help older adults and users with certain disabilities communicate efficiently.

> [!failure] The Bottleneck
> Despite these advancements, our communication forms remain somewhat primitive. If you have to walk up to a wall-sized display and type out commands using a traditional keyboard, you lose the feeling of ubiquity.
> 
> Q: What is the current limitation of ubiquitous computing technologies?
> A: A lack of efficient text input interfaces currently limits the usefulness of ubiquitous computing technologies.

### Why is traditional typing a bottleneck?
* Humans have 10 fingers.
* The brain takes several hundred milliseconds to send a nerve pulse to the finger to press a key.
* A single keystroke takes at least 500 milliseconds.
* This creates a theoretical upper bound of typing speed (around 100-120 words per minute for experts), while system speeds are increasing by magnitudes.

## 4. Case Study: The Gesture Keyboard (ShapeWriter)

To solve the text input bottleneck, researchers had to ask the right questions about the physics of typing. 

![[Pasted image 20260330002935.png]]

> [!question] Identifying the Overhead
> What limits a typing input method? The interval of raising the finger and finding the next key. The time the finger is in the air is pure <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #e67e22;">overhead</span>. Traditional keyboards were based on mechanical typewriters which required this motion.

> [!check] The Solution: Elimination of Keystrokes
> To increase input rate, we must eliminate finger raising and pressing. The solution is a sliding platform where you drag your fingers across the surface. This eliminates muscle strain, reduces fatigue, and drastically cuts down the time between letters.

> [!faq] Q: What is a "Gesture Keyboard" and what does it do?
> A: A gesture keyboard is a mobile text entry method that exploits language redundancies to amplify human text input. By tracing lines between letters (like spelling the word "speech"), it allows for a continuous transition from novice to expert behavior.

> [!faq] Q: How fast can users type with a gesture keyboard?
> A: Gesture keyboards enable users to write text at 25-40 words per minute (wpm), with record speeds reaching up to 99 wpm for individual phrases.

### History and Commercialization
> [!faq] Q: What is the history and commercial trajectory of the gesture keyboard?
> A: Originally known as SHARK, SHARK2, and IBM SharkText, it had its first public release on IBM alphaWorks in 2004. It was later commercialized as ShapeWriter, Swype, and T9 Trace/Flext9, with ShapeWriter, Inc. eventually being acquired by Nuance in 2010.

### The Skill Acquisition Loop
Gesture keyboards are brilliant because they support user learning naturally.

> [!faq] Q: How does the skill acquisition cycle work for users of gesture keyboards?
> A: The keyboard allows for a continuous learning loop driven by consistent movement patterns:
> * <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #3498db;">Complete novice:</span> Users trace from letter to letter in a closed-loop manner, which is slow and accurate.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #2ecc71;">Complete expert:</span> Users gesture word shapes in an open-loop manner, which is fast and inaccurate.
> * Users can fall back and relearn as needed during the process.

> [!info] Additional Note: Fitts's Law in Action
> The mechanics of the gesture keyboard elegantly bypass the constraints outlined by Fitts's Law. Fitts's Law states that the time required to move to a target is a function of the distance to the target and the size of the target. By dragging the finger rather than lifting and searching (which resets the pointing task for every single letter), gesture typing transforms a series of discrete, high-cognitive-load pointing tasks into a single, continuous, low-cognitive-load spatial memory task.

## 5. Current Challenges in Intelligent Interactive Systems

Even the gesture keyboard may soon become obsolete as we move toward voice-based interaction (voice translation, voice coding) and AI personal assistants. 

> [!faq] Q: What are the current challenges when designing intelligent interactive systems?
> A: There are three main challenges highlighted:
> 1. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Flexible multimodal interaction:</span> There is a need for multimodal signal processing algorithms that seamlessly fuse multiple text input modalities, such as eye-trackers, speech, and gestures. Systems also need flexible unimodal and multimodal error correction methods, and they must exploit the rich hypothesis spaces of underlying recognition algorithms to be efficient.
> 2. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Context modelling:</span> To improve performance, sensor technologies must be combined with user task modelling to accurately infer the user's context.
> 3. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Leveraging crowdsourcing and social media:</span> There is a need to explore how to further leverage crowdsourcing and social media to improve statistical language modelling.

> [!important] Deep Dive into the Challenges
> 
> * <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">Context Modeling Example:</span> Imagine hiring an AI assistant. Just like a human CEO assistant, the AI needs to understand your context - knowing when to interrupt you and when to handle things silently. Can we trust these AI systems in high-stakes environments like medical interventions? That is a profound HCI question.
> * <span style="color: #d35400; font-weight: bold;">Statistics & Correlation vs Causation:</span> When presenting data, framing is everything. You might see a statistic stating a COVID vaccine reduces spread by 67%. Conversely, an HCI study might show that playing an addictive single-player game for 8 hours a day reduces spread by 55% (simply because the user never leaves the house). Asking the right questions helps interpret these behavioral statistics accurately.
> * <span style="color: #27ae60; font-weight: bold;">Crowdsourcing Power:</span> Language modeling is incredibly difficult. For a language like Sinhala (22 million speakers), crowdsourcing could refine the entire language model if everyone participates. For English, the scale is in the billions. This scale of user-generated input is a massive frontier for HCI.

> [!caution] Additional Note: Usable Security and Context
> When implementing context modeling and ubiquitous systems, cybersecurity must be considered a core component of the HCI process. Highly secure systems - like strict vulnerability scanning layers or complex cryptographic integrations - often fail in practice if the HCI is abrasive. If security measures slow down the user too much, users will find unsafe workarounds. Designing "usable security" is one of the most pressing challenges where HCI and network security intersect today.

## 6. HCI Research Areas & The Future

The boundaries of HCI are constantly expanding. What used to be simple screen interactions have evolved into complex, multi-layered environments encompassing everything from hardware to social structures.

> [!faq] Q: What are some examples of HCI research areas?
> A: Some active research areas include information visualisation and surfaces, pervasive and ubiquitous computing, multiple displays (shared and extended displays), privacy and social challenges in HCI, and human-machine interfacing.

> [!faq] Q: What is "E-conic"?
> A: E-conic is an example of HCI research involving a Perspective-aware MDE (Multi-Display Environment).

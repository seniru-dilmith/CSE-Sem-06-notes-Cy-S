## 1. The Landscape of Design Rules

Designing for usability is not a random act; it involves applying different levels of rules. These rules are categorized based on two main axes: <span style="color:#3498db; font-weight:bold;">Authority</span> and <span style="color:#e67e22; font-weight:bold;">Generality</span>.

> [!info] The Dual Axes
> - <span style="font-weight:bold;">Increasing Generality:</span> How broadly a rule can be applied across different systems and domains.
> - <span style="font-weight:bold;">Increasing Authority:</span> The level of "control power" or strictness a rule carries (e.g., must follow vs. suggested to follow).

### Categorization Table

| Rule Type | Authority | Generality | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <span style="color:#9b59b6; font-weight:bold;">Principles</span> | Low | High | Abstract rules providing general understanding. |
| <span style="color:#3498db; font-weight:bold;">Guidelines</span> | Medium/Low | Medium/High | Suggestive rules providing direction for design. |
| <span style="color:#e74c3c; font-weight:bold;">Standards</span> | High | Low | Specific rules that must be followed for compliance. |

> [!question] FAQ: The Goal and Types
> 
> Q: What is the primary goal of interaction design, and what are the main types of design rules used to achieve it?
> 
> A: The core goal of interaction design is designing for maximum usability. To accomplish this, designers rely on three main types of design rules:
> 
> - Principles: Abstract rules; low authority but high generality.
>     
> - Guidelines: Direction for design; lower authority but general application.
>     
> - Standards: Specific rules; high authority but limited application.

## 2. Core Principles to Support Usability

Principles are the highest level of abstraction. They don't tell you exactly where to put a button, but they orient your mindset toward a "good" design.

> [!quote]
> 
> "Preventing errors is a principle." It applies to photocopy machines, cooking appliances, vehicles, and software alike.

> [!question] FAQ: The Three Core Principles
> 
> Q: What are the three core principles used to support usability?
> 
> A: The three core principles are Learnability, Flexibility, and Robustness.

### A. Learnability

The ease with which new users can begin effective interaction and achieve maximal performance.

> [!example] Use Case: Cutting a Tree Branch
> 
> Whether you use a manual saw, a chainsaw, or a remote-controlled robot, the core process remains the same: identify the branch, ensure safety, cut, and dispose. Learnability is about how easily a user maps this real-world process into the system's interface.

#### Specific Components of Learnability:

- Predictability: Determining the effect of future actions based on past history. Relies heavily on operation visibility.
    
- Synthesizability: Assessing the effect of past actions. This involves "honesty" — whether the system shows the result immediately or eventually.
    
- Familiarity: How prior knowledge applies to a new system.
    
    - Guessability & Affordance: Can the user take a "happy risk" and guess how something works?
        
    - Real-world Mapping: Using colors (like flag colors for countries) or standard icons to trigger recognition.
        
- Generalizability: Extending interaction knowledge from one situation to another (e.g., if Ctrl+P works for one document, it should work for another).
    
- Consistency: Likeness in behavior for similar tasks.
    
    - Crucial Note: Inconsistency causes cognitive load. Even a one-pixel difference can be jarring to a user.
        

> [!question] FAQ: Defining Learnability
> 
> Q: What specific principles define "Learnability"?
> 
> A: Learnability relies on: Predictability, Synthesizability, Familiarity, Generalizability, and Consistency.

### B. Flexibility

The multiplicity of ways the user and system exchange information.

> [!warning] Caution: The Balance of Control
> 
> Users love control but hate effort. If a system forces a user to perform redundant tasks (like re-entering a password 10 times), it fails at flexibility.

#### Specific Components of Flexibility:

- Dialogue Initiative: Freedom from system-imposed constraints. Is the system pre-emptive or the user?
    
- Multithreading: Supporting more than one task at a time (concurrent vs. interleaving).
    
- Task Migratability: Passing responsibility between user and system.
    
    - Example: An automated signature process is better than manually dragging a signature box onto every page of a 100-page PDF.
        
- Substitutivity: Allowing equivalent values of input/output to be substituted (e.g., using a slider vs. typing a number).
    
- Customizability: Modifiability by the user (adaptability) or the system (adaptivity).
    

> [!question] FAQ: Defining Flexibility
> 
> Q: What specific principles define "Flexibility"?
> 
> A: Flexibility is supported by: Dialogue initiative, Multithreading, Task migratability, Substitutivity, and Customizability.

### C. Robustness

The level of support provided to help the user achieve and assess goal-directed behavior.

#### Specific Components of Robustness:

- Observability: Can the user see what's happening inside?
    
    - Example: If an installer doesn't show a progress bar, the user might check Task Manager to see if the CPU is still working — that is a failure of system observability.
        
- Recoverability: Ability to take corrective action.
    
    - Important: If an online form wipes all data after one validation error, it has zero recoverability.
        
- Responsiveness: How the user perceives the rate of communication.
    
- Task Conformance: Does the system support all the user's tasks? (Task completeness and adequacy).
    

> [!question] FAQ: Defining Robustness
> 
> Q: What specific principles define "Robustness"?
> 
> A: Robustness involves: Observability, Recoverability, Responsiveness, and Task conformance.

## 3. Standards and Guidelines

When we move from abstract principles to practical application, we encounter Standards and Guidelines.

### Standards

- Source: National or international bodies (ISO).
    
- Nature: High authority, low level of detail.
    
- Requirement: Sound underlying theory and slowly changing technology.
    
- Example: ISO 9241 defines usability as effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction.
    

### Guidelines

- Source: Textbooks, style guides (e.g., Apple's Human Interface Guidelines).
    
- Nature: Suggestive and general.
    
- Application: Abstract guidelines are used early in the lifecycle; detailed style guides are used later.
    
- Utility: Crucial for resolving design conflicts between team members.
    

> [!question] FAQ: Standards vs. Guidelines
> 
> Q: What are the characteristics of design "Standards"?
> 
> A: Standards are set by bodies to ensure strict compliance. They require sound theory and slow-changing tech. Hardware standards are more common than software. **ISO 9241** is a key example.
> 
> Q: How are "Guidelines" utilized in the design process?
> 
> A: Guidelines are suggestive and general. Abstract ones apply early (principles), while detailed ones (style guides) apply later. They aid in resolving conflicts.

## 4. Golden Rules and Heuristics

These are "broad brush" design rules that act as checklists. As the lecture states: "Better design using these than using nothing!"

### Shneiderman’s 8 Golden Rules

1. Strive for consistency.
    
2. Enable frequent users to use shortcuts.
    
3. Offer informative feedback.
    
4. Design dialogs to yield closure.
    
5. Offer error prevention and simple error handling.
    
6. Permit easy reversal of actions.
    
7. Support internal locus of control.
    
8. Reduce short-term memory load.
    

### Norman’s 7 Principles

1. Use both knowledge in the world and knowledge in the head.
    
2. Simplify the structure of tasks.
    
3. Make things visible (bridge the gulfs of Execution and Evaluation).
    
4. Get the mappings right.
    
5. Exploit the power of constraints (natural and artificial).
    
6. Design for error - assume users make errors always and design such that the system doesn't allow it
    
7. When all else fails, standardize.


> [!tip]
> 
> Natural Constraints: Humans have two eyes, ten fingers, and limited short-term memory ($7 \pm 2$ items). Good design exploits these limits.

## 5. HCI Design Patterns

A pattern is an **invariant solution to a recurrent problem within a specific context**.

> [!info] Origin
> 
> Originated in architecture with Christopher Alexander (e.g., "Light on Two Sides of Every Room"). In HCI, an example is "Go back to a safe place."

### Why Patterns are Effective:

- They capture design practice, not just theory.
    
- They are linked in "languages" to create complete designs.
    
- They are generative — they help build the whole system.
    
- They express what is humane in interface design.
    

> [!question] FAQ: HCI Design Patterns
> 
> Q: What are HCI design patterns and where did they originate?
> 
> A: They are an approach to reusing successful design solutions. They originated in architecture. A pattern is an invariant solution to a recurrent problem.
> 
> Q: What makes design patterns effective for teams?
> 
> A: They capture practice over theory, represent knowledge at varying levels, embody humane values, and are intuitive for communication among stakeholders.

## 6. Achieving Repeatable Design

> [!important] The Success Formula
> 
> Success = Creative Insight (New Paradigms) + Purposeful Principled Practice (Standards/Guidelines).

### The Evolution of Web Design

- Mid-90s/Early 2000s: "Carnival" websites with rainbow colors, blinking text, and chaotic fonts. Highly creative but a nightmare for usability.
    
- Modern Era: Discipline through CSS themes and design patterns. Consistency makes it easier to learn and predict behavior across the web.
    
> [!question] FAQ: Repeatable Design
> 
> Q: How can designers consistently achieve repeatable design for usability?
> 
> A: By abstracting general properties from good designs. Success requires a blend of creative insight and purposeful principled practice.

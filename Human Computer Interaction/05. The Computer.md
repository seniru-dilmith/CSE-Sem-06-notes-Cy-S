## 1. Interactivity and System Elements

A computer system is not just a box on a desk; it is an ecosystem of elements that dictate how we interact with digital spaces.

> [!question] <span style="font-weight: bold">Q: What are the main elements of a computer system that affect human-computer interaction?</span>
> <span style="font-weight: bold">A:</span> A computer system comprises <span style="font-weight: bold">input devices</span> (for text entry and pointing), <span style="font-weight: bold">output devices</span> (like screens and digital paper), <span style="font-weight: bold">virtual reality</span> interaction and display devices, <span style="font-weight: bold">physical interaction</span> devices (such as sound, haptics, and bio-sensors), <span style="font-weight: bold">paper</span> (for print and scan), <span style="font-weight: bold">memory</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold">processing speed and networks</span>.

### The Evolution of Interactivity
Historically, humans interacted with computers via <mark style="background-color: #ffd700">batch processing</mark>. Users would prepare punched cards or large data files, experience a long wait, and eventually receive line printer output. There was no real-time engagement. 

Today, computing is defined by <span style="color: #2e8b57">rapid transactions and interactive feedback</span>. The user remains in control, doing rather than waiting. However, this raises an important design philosophy question:

> [!quote] <span style="font-style: italic">Is faster always better?</span>
> In the past, the lack of real-time processing forced users to think, design, and optimize carefully before executing tasks. Today, rapid interactions can sometimes lead to mindless button-pressing without deep thought. A core HCI challenge is determining how society utilizes the free time gained by computational efficiency.

## 2. Text Entry Devices

Text remains the primary form of human-computer interaction. This is largely a historical artifact: computers were initially adopted as more efficient replacements for typewriters, particularly for government and official communications.

> [!question] Q: What are the different types of keyboard layouts and their pros and cons?
> 
> A: The QWERTY layout is standardized and common but not optimal for typing, as it was originally designed to prevent early typewriters from jamming. The Alphabetic layout is not faster for either trained typists or beginners. The Dvorak layout places common letters under dominant fingers, offering a 10-15% improvement in speed and reduced fatigue, but resistance from the large base of QWERTY typists restricts its adoption. There are also special keyboards, like the Maltron left-handed keyboard designed to reduce repetitive strain injury (RSI). Finally, Chord keyboards use only 4 or 5 keys where letters are typed as combinations; they are fast and compact but face social resistance and cause fatigue after extended use.

### The History of QWERTY

> [!note] Why do we still use QWERTY?
> 
> The QWERTY layout was born out of mechanical necessity. When the printing press (invented by Gutenberg to scale up Bible production) evolved into the typewriter, fast typists caused adjacent mechanical hammers to collide and jam. Statisticians studied the English language to separate frequently used letter pairs. QWERTY deliberately placed common characters far apart to slow down the mechanical interference.
> 
> Even though modern computers lack mechanical hammers, we still use QWERTY because people resist change. Society has invested massive effort into acquiring QWERTY muscle memory.

### Mobile Text Entry

> [!question] Q: How is text entered on mobile phones?
> 
> A: Phones use numeric pads requiring multiple key presses for letters (e.g., pressing '2' three times for 'c'). They also use T9 predictive entry, which allows typing with a single key press per letter, relying on a dictionary to 'guess' the intended word.
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260422010515.png]]

T9 Speed Phenomenon: T9 entry is surprisingly fast because the relative distance between finger movements is essentially zero. Once your finger rests on a key, subsequent presses require no travel time (Fitts' Law application).

### Numpad Layout Differences

> [!question] Q: How do numeric keypads differ between devices?
> 
> A: The layout of numbers on an ATM or telephone is inverted compared to a calculator or PC keyboard; a telephone places the 1, 2, and 3 keys on the top row, while a calculator places the 7, 8, and 9 on the top row.
![[Pasted image 20260422010545.png]]

> [!tip] Why the difference?
> 
> Calculators evolved from heavy financial work where "0" and the decimal point were the most frequently used keys (e.g., typing cents). Thus, the lower numbers were placed closer to the bottom. Telephones adopted the 1-2-3 top row simply to follow standard reading order.

### Handwriting and Speech Recognition

> [!question] Q: What are the technical challenges associated with handwriting and speech recognition?
> 
> A: For handwriting recognition, technical problems include naturally capturing useful information (like stroke path and pressure), segmenting joined-up writing, interpreting individual letters, and coping with varying handwriting styles. For speech recognition, problems stem from external noise interference, imprecise pronunciation, large vocabularies, and varying speakers, making the technology currently most successful for single users with limited vocabularies.

Algorithmic Shift in Handwriting: Early handwriting recognition failed because it relied on pixel-to-pixel matching. If someone drew an "A" slightly wider, the computer failed to recognize it. Modern systems succeed by using line-based vector recognition - analyzing the angles and connections of lines rather than literal pixel placement.

## 3. Positioning, Pointing, and Drawing

Interaction requires spatial navigation across the screen's coordinate system.

> [!question] Q: What are the characteristics of a computer mouse and how does it detect motion?
> 
> A: A mouse is an indirect manipulation device that relies on planar movement and buttons, translating movement in the (x, z) desktop plane to cursor movement in the (x, y) screen plane. A mechanical mouse uses a ball that rotates orthogonal potentiometers to track movement, while an optical mouse uses a light-emitting diode (LED) to detect fluctuating alterations in reflected light intensity to calculate relative motion.

> [!question] Q: What are some alternative devices to the mouse for pointing and positioning?
> 
> A:
> 
> - Touchpads: Small tablets where 'acceleration' settings are critical for balancing fast strokes and slow precision.
>     
> - Trackballs: Upside-down mice with static housing, fast for gaming.
>     
> - Thumbwheels: Accurate dial controls for CAD or scrolling.
>     
> - Joysticks & Keyboard Nipples: Use indirect pressure/velocity mapping.
>     
> - Touch-sensitive screens: Direct pointing, great for hostile environments but imprecise.
>     
> - Styluses and Light Pens: Direct drawing, though they can obscure the screen.
>     
> - Eyegaze systems: Track retina reflections using a low-power laser.
>     
> - Cursor keys: Very cheap but slow basic motion.
>     
> - Discrete positioning controls: Mini-joysticks for simple TV/phone menus.
>     

### The Inverted-T Cursor Logic

Cursor keys usually feature an inverted "T" shape (Up arrow on top, Left/Down/Right below). This design aligns with human reading habits. Because we read from top to bottom, our most frequent movements are downward and sideways. The inverted T minimizes the finger travel distance for these specific actions.

## 4. Display Devices

Displays are grids of colored dots (pixels). Quality depends on:

1. Resolution: Measured in dimensions (e.g., 1024x768) or density (dpi).
    
2. Aspect Ratio: Width vs. height (4:3 for old monitors, 16:9 for modern screens).
    
3. Color Depth: The number of possible colors per pixel.
    

> [!question] Q: How do bitmap display devices represent images and what dictates their quality?
> 
> A: Bitmap displays use a vast number of colored dots. Display quality is determined by resolution, aspect ratio, and color depth. To prevent "jaggies" (discontinuities on diagonal lines), screens use anti-aliasing to soften edges by utilizing intermediate shades of the line color.

### Anti-Aliasing Explained

Because screens use rigid x/y coordinate pixels, diagonal lines naturally look like jagged staircases ("jaggies"). Anti-aliasing fools human vision by placing less contrasting, lighter-colored pixels along the edges of the line. The naked eye blends these pale border pixels, perceiving a smooth line, even though the line is technically rendered thicker.

> [!question] Q: What is the difference between CRT and LCD displays?
> 
> A: A Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) uses an electron gun and magnetic fields to direct an electron beam onto a phosphor-coated screen that glows. Liquid Crystal Displays (LCDs) are smaller, lighter, and emit no radiation. LCDs work by passing light through polarized plates and crystals; applying voltage changes the polarization to alter colors, and because light is reflected rather than emitted, they cause less eye strain.
![[Pasted image 20260422010734.png]]
### Future Displays: Situated and Digital Paper

> [!question] Q: What are situated displays and digital paper?
> 
> A: Situated displays are screens placed in specific physical contexts, such as the Hermes system which places small displays next to office doors for leaving handwritten notes. Digital paper consists of thin, flexible sheets that are updated electronically but retain their display without continuous power, functioning via rotating microscopic colored spheres or colored liquids.

Digital Paper Mechanics: In prototypes, digital paper features tiny cylinders filled with an ink droplet. When an electrical charge is applied, the ink rises to the surface, turning the pixel black. Controlling the height of the ink creates grayscale. By utilizing three cylinders (RGB), full-color foldable newspapers could become a reality.

## 5. Virtual Reality and 3D Interaction

Virtual reality immerses the user by tracking 3D physical movement (pitch, yaw, and roll).
![[Pasted image 20260422010835.png]]
> [!question] Q: What technologies are used for Virtual Reality (VR) and 3D interaction?
> 
> A: 3D positioning utilizes cockpit virtual controls, 3D mice, data gloves utilizing fiber optics, VR helmets for head motion tracking, and whole-body tracking. VR headsets provide a 3D effect by showing a small TV screen to each eye at slightly different angles. Simulators and VR caves project scenes on walls and use real controls, other people, and hydraulic rams to create realistic environments.

> [!question] Q: What causes VR motion sickness?
> 
> A: VR sickness is caused by conflicting sensory cues, such as a time delay between physical head movement and the display updating, or a conflict in depth perception where the headset implies 3D stereo distance but the user's eyes are focused on a single flat plane.

> [!danger] The Biological Trigger for VR Sickness
> 
> Motion sickness in VR is an evolutionary defense mechanism. When your eyes tell your brain you are free-falling from a roller coaster, but your inner ear and feet report that you are standing perfectly still on solid ground, the brain's logic centers short-circuit. In nature, such severe sensory dissonance usually means you have ingested a neurotoxin. The brain responds by inducing nausea to purge the stomach, causing the user to feel violently sick.

## 6. Physical Controls and Sensors

> [!question] Q: What types of physical controls and sensors are used in user interfaces?
> 
> A: Interfaces utilize dedicated displays (analogue dials, digital LCDs, head-up displays), sounds for warnings and confirmations, and haptic devices that provide vibration and force feedback (e.g., BMW iDrive). Additionally, environmental sensors (ultrasound, RFID) and bio-sensors (iris scanners, heart rate monitors, galvanic skin response) read physical data.

### UI Grouping in the Physical World

Consider the physical controls on a microwave. The most effective designs group similar functionalities together. For instance, weight settings have one button shape, food types have another, and heating modes have a third. This physical grouping principle allows users to quickly build a mental model of the interface.

## 7. Printing, Scanning, and Paper Interfaces

> [!question] Q: How do different printers work and what are their critical features?
> 
> A: Printers create images from small dots; their critical features are resolution (dpi), speed, and cost.
> 
> - Dot-matrix printers use a line of pins striking an inked ribbon (80-120 dpi).
>     
> - Ink-jet printers shoot tiny blobs of ink onto paper (300+ dpi).
>     
> - Laser printers use an electrostatic drum to pick up toner powder, fixed with heat (600+ dpi).
>     

> [!question] Q: What is a font and how is page layout managed for printing?
> 
> A: A font is a specific style of text, which can be fixed-pitch (equal width) or variable-pitched, and can be serif (splayed ends) or sans-serif (square-ended). A Page Description Language (like PostScript) is used to send instructions for shapes and text to the printer rather than sending a massive bitmap file.

> [!tip] Serif vs. Sans-Serif Rule of Thumb
> 
> - Sans-Serif (e.g., Helvetica): Best for overhead presentations. They offer clean, bold messages without visual distraction.
>     
> - Serif (e.g., Times New Roman): Best for long paragraphs. The tiny "split ends" (serifs) act as visual resting points, guiding the eye smoothly across the line and reducing reading fatigue.
>     

> [!question] Q: How is physical paper converted back into digital data?
> 
> A: Scanners convert the page into a bitmap. Optical Character Recognition (OCR) systems then attempt to convert that bitmap back into text, though they frequently struggle with varying fonts and complex page formats.

A Note on Scanning Resolution: Scanning at 2400 dpi is not inherently better than 300 dpi for a standard document. High resolutions will perfectly capture every paper fiber, dust particle, and printer ink impurity, resulting in massive, noisy files. High dpi is only useful for archaeological artifacts or currency inspection.

## 8. Data Storage and Compression

> [!question] Q: What is the difference between lossless and lossy compression?
> 
> A: Lossless compression (e.g., GIF, ZIP) looks for data commonalities to reduce storage size while allowing the exact recovery of the original file. Lossy compression (e.g., JPEG, MP3) exploits human perception to discard less noticeable information (like rapid visual changes or drowned-out audio notes), recovering a file similar to the original.

> [!question] Q: What are the common storage formats for text and media?
> 
> A: Text formats include ASCII, UTF-8, RTF, SGML, and XML. Media formats include PostScript, GIF, JPEG, TIFF, and PICT for images, and QuickTime, MPEG, and WAV for audio and video.

## 9. Processing Speed and UI Limitations

> [!question] Q: What are the limitations on interactive performance, and how do they relate to Moore's Law?
> 
> A: Moore's Law observed that processor speed doubles every 18 months and memory capacity doubles every 12 months. However, interactive performance is still limited by bottlenecks: computation bounds (slow processing), storage channel bounds (slow disk transfer), graphics bounds (heavy display updates), and slow network capacity.

### The User Experience Impact

When processing falls behind human input, users experience severe frustration.

- Buffered Keypresses: The system lags, the user holds down the cursor key, and once the system catches up, the cursor wildly overshoots the target.
    
- Icon Wars: A user clicks an icon, nothing happens. They click three more times in frustration. When the CPU finally unfreezes, four windows explode onto the screen simultaneously.
    

> [!warning] Designing for the Lowest Common Denominator
> 
> While network bandwidth has drastically improved over the last decade, content has become proportionally heavier (e.g., 1080p autoplay videos). Because you cannot control the end-user's hardware, never assume they have a fast connection or a modern device. Designing clean, lightweight interfaces ensures a world-class UI experience regardless of the user's computational limitations.

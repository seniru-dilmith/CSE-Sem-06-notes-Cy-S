> [!abstract] Overview
> Interaction design goes far beyond merely throwing buttons on a screen. It is fundamentally about designing <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #2980b9;">interventions</span> that adapt to human psychology and physical limitations. This comprehensive note bridges core HCI concepts with practical interface design guidelines, balancing utility, aesthetics, and human unpredictability.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> Good design treats human users as imperfect, irrational materials and builds protective, intuitive constraints around them. By relying on personas, scenarios, logical layouts, and cultural affordances, designers can create systems that users seamlessly integrate into their linear, daily lives.

## 1. The Concept of Design and Interventions

To begin designing, we must first understand what "design" actually entails within the context of Human-Computer Interaction. 

> [!question] What is the main focus of interaction design?
> [!faq] Answer
> Interaction design involves designing interventions, not just immediate interfaces or artifacts. This means considering how technology changes interaction styles, such as moving from a manual to an electric stapler, and including elements like documentation, manuals, tutorials, and what we say and do.

> [!question] How is "design" defined in this context?
> [!faq] Answer
> Design is defined as achieving goals within constraints. It requires understanding the purpose (who it is for and why they want it), the constraints (materials and platforms), and making necessary trade-offs.

### The Golden Rule: Understand Your Materials
In physical engineering, a sculptor understands clay, and an architect understands concrete. In UI/UX, our "materials" are two-fold:
1. <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #8e44ad;">Computers & Platforms:</span> Understanding the limitations of the web, Android, Windows, or iOS.
2. <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #27ae60;">Human Beings:</span> Understanding biological, psychological, and social constraints.

> [!question] What is the "golden rule" of design?
> [!faq] Answer
> The golden rule is to understand your materials. For human-computer interaction, this means understanding computers (their limitations, capacities, tools, and platforms), understanding people (psychological and social aspects, including human error), and how the two interact.

### The Irrational Human User
Unlike computers, humans are biologically "irrational" from a strict systems perspective. In fields like applied sciences and mathematics, theoretical models assume users behave perfectly. In the real world, humans act unpredictably.

> [!quote] The Reality of User Behavior
> <span style="font-style: italic;">"Every given opportunity, they make a mistake."</span>

> [!question] How should designers approach human error?
> [!faq] Answer
> Designers should recognize that human "error" is normal behavior under stress. Just as an engineer knows how concrete behaves under weight and designs around it rather than blaming a "lintel error," a designer should understand how users behave under stress and design for it, treating users at least as well as physical materials.

> [!example] The Staircase Analogy
> If you have a small child in a house with a staircase, you do not expect the child to rationally avoid the stairs. You <span style="background-color: #f39c12; color: #fff; padding: 2px; border-radius: 3px;">put a fence</span>. Similarly, constraints in software should be like that fence. The trick is to implement checks and balances behind the scenes so the user is protected <span style="font-weight: bold;">without</span> feeling annoyed or strictly monitored.

## 2. The Design Process

The life cycle of interaction design differs significantly from traditional static requirement engineering (like a standard UML Use Case).

```mermaid
graph LR
    A[Requirements] --> B[Analysis]
    B --> C[Design]
    C --> D[Prototyping & Iteration]
    D --> E[Implementation/Deploy]
    D -.->|Feedback| B
````

> [!question] What are the key steps in the design process?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> The design process includes determining requirements (what is there vs. what is wanted), analysis (ordering and understanding), design (what to do and how to decide), iteration and prototyping (finding out what is really needed), and implementation and deployment.

### Development vs. Interaction Load

Historically, with tools like Java Swing, the developer's cognitive load was entirely consumed by managing layouts, panels, and event listeners. The developer spent hours just rendering the UI, ignoring the actual _interaction_. Modern frameworks shift this paradigm, allowing us to focus on how the user achieves their happy path and how we handle deviations.

> [!question] How does limited time impact design and usability?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Limited time forces design trade-offs. Designers must focus on finding usability problems and deciding which ones to fix. Consequently, a "perfect system" is actually badly designed, because making it too good implies too much effort was wasted in the design phase.

> [!failure] The Perfect System Fallacy
> 
> A "perfect" system on paper is a failed design if it took too long to build. Sometimes a compromised version built via quick prototyping serves the real-world user far better than an over-engineered masterclass.

## 3. Understanding Users (Personas & Probes)

Users are not stakeholders in the traditional business sense, and you are not the user. Developers possess abstract-thinking mindsets; standard users often operate with very concrete, goal-oriented mindsets under different life pressures.

> [!question] What is a "persona" and how is it used?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> A persona is a detailed description of an "example" user. While not necessarily a real person, adding specific details (like age, job history, family, and physical traits) makes the persona feel real so designers can use them as a surrogate user to evaluate how they would react to a system.

### Detailed Persona Breakdown: "Betty"

A proper persona is not a generic stick-figure. Let's analyze a typical, highly-detailed persona profile:

> [!example] Persona: Betty, 37
> 
> Age/Role: 37 years old, warehouse manager for 5 years.
> 
> Education: Did not go to university, has a basic business diploma. (Implication: Avoid overly abstract, complex tech jargon.)
> 
> Family: Two children, aged 15 and 7. (Implication: She is busy. She refuses to work late. Your system must be efficient, or she will reject it.)
> 
> Physicality: Right-hand movement is restricted due to an industrial accident. (Implication: Heavy reliance on precise mouse-clicking or rapid typing will severely frustrate her.)
> 
> Attitude: Feels threatened by new computer systems. (Implication: The system starts on unfavorable ground. It must build trust immediately.)

> [!info] Biological & Demographic details matter
> 
> Unlike back-end software engineering where the "Admin" role is just an abstract entity with permissions, an interaction designer must know if the user is middle-aged, their eyesight quality, or their daily stress levels.

> [!question] What are "cultural probes"?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Because direct observation is sometimes difficult, designers use probe packs (containing items like a camera, postcard, or a glass to listen at a wall) to prompt responses from users in their own environments. Users record what is meaningful to them, which helps inform interviews, prompt ideas, and enculture designers.

> [!tip] Practical Probe Usage
> 
> Give users a bag with a stapler, post-its, and a camera. Observe where they place them on their desks. This reveals their behavioral traits and organizational preferences.

## 4. Scenarios

Scenarios take the Persona and place them into motion. They are linear step-by-step walkthroughs.

> [!question] How do scenarios assist in interaction design?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Scenarios are rich stories used to communicate with others, validate models, and understand dynamics. They involve step-by-step walkthroughs of what users want to do, detailing what they see, what they do, and what they are thinking. Designers might even play-act scenarios using mock-up devices to explore physical interaction, user cognition, and system architecture.

> [!hint] Play-Acting Vulnerability
> 
> Getting users to "play act" with prototypes, especially vulnerable users transitioning from manual systems, turns them into advocates for your system rather than complainers.

### Exploring Physical Interactions

> [!error] The Swiss Knife Design Flaw
> 
> Imagine designing a digital interface on a Swiss Army Knife and expecting the user to use the plastic toothpick as a stylus. When you play-act this, you immediately realize the user's thumb naturally rests directly over the touchscreen to grip the knife. The design fails in reality!

> [!question] What is a drawback of using scenarios?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Scenarios are linear because time and human lives are linear, making them natural and easy to understand. However, this linearity means they lack choice, branches, and special conditions, potentially causing designers to miss unintended outcomes. To fix this, designers should use several scenarios and multiple methods.

> [!bug] The Linearity Trap
> 
> Because human time is linear, we prefer straight A-to-B paths. However, complex systems have alternative paths. If a user branches off, scenarios might fail to account for the UI breakage. Always use multiple scenarios.

## 5. Navigation Design

Navigation is how the user moves through the ecosystem you have built. At any given moment, a user perceives the single screen they are looking at as the entire system.

> [!question] What are the four levels of navigation design?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Navigation involves widget choice (menus, buttons), screen design, application navigation design (local and global structure), and the broader environment (other applications and the OS).

### Local Navigation

When viewing a single screen, you must eliminate the feeling of being lost.

> [!question] What are the four golden rules for goal seeking in local navigation?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> To help users navigate from one screen to their goal, the interface should help them know:
> 
> 1. where they are (e.g., using breadcrumbs),
>     
> 2. what they can do,
>     
> 3. where they are going or what will happen next, and
>     
> 4. where they have been or what they have done.
>     

> [!check] Best Practice: Splitting Complex Tasks
> 
> Do not force users to do profile configuration and registration on the same screen. Break them up. Let users feel rewarded for completing a small task, which motivates them to tackle the next.

### Global Navigation & Modes

How do we map the grand architecture?

> [!question] How should global application structure be mapped?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> It can be mapped using hierarchical diagrams to show parts of an application separated by function, though deep hierarchies are difficult to navigate and often inappropriately apply Miller’s 7 ± 2 rule to menu size instead of short-term memory. Network diagrams are also used to show task-oriented paths, what leads to what, what happens when, and how branches operate.

Code snippet

```
graph TD
    A[Home] --> B[Dashboard]
    A --> C[Settings]
    B --> D[Reports]
    B --> E[Analytics]
    C --> F[User Profile]
    C --> G[Preferences]
    E -.->|Network Path| F
```

> [!important] Miller's Law Misapplication
> 
> Many developers restrict menus to 7 items because of Miller's 7±2 rule. This is a misapplication! The rule applies to short-term memory, not visual scanning. To support memory, do not force users to re-enter data they provided on previous screens. Use a sidebar to display a cached miniature version of their previous inputs.

> [!question] What are the risks associated with "modes" in physical devices?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Modes, such as a lock to prevent accidental phone use, can cause critical errors. If a user forgets the phone is unlocked and presses buttons while it is in their pocket, an action intended for unlocking might accidentally navigate the phone book and delete an entry.

> [!danger] Mode Failures
> 
> If the same physical input acts entirely differently depending on a hidden "state" or "mode", you risk catastrophic user errors.

## 6. Screen Design, Layout, and Dialogue

> [!question] What does "dialogue" mean in UI design?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Dialogue represents the pattern of interaction between users and the system. Similar to a marriage service, the general pattern and flow remain the same, but specific details (like names or data entered) differ each time.

> [!question] What basic principles guide screen layout and entering information?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Designers should ask what the user is doing, think about natural order and comparisons, and group items so that things that belong logically together are placed physically together. Form follows function, and visual tools like white space, alignment, grouping, and decoration are used to achieve this. Screen layout should also account for the active vs. passive elements of forms so users know what to do and where to type.

### The Cake Recipe Syndrome

> [!question] What is the "cake recipe syndrome"?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> This is an error in instructional order where a step is mentioned out of sequence, much like a recipe telling you to mix milk and flour, and add fruit "after beating them". Screen order must match the natural order of actions.

### Alignment and Typography

> [!question] What are the best practices for alignment and multiple columns?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> - Text: Because we read left to right, text should be left-aligned.
>     
> - Names: Align names to make scanning for surnames easy.
>     
> - Numbers: Align decimal points or right-align integers so that visually longer numbers correspond to mathematically larger numbers.
>     
> - Columns: Scanning across gaps is hard, so designers should use leaders, greying, or deliberately poor alignment to help the eye track across the data.
>     

> [!note] Visual Fixation Points
> 
> If data is unaligned, the human eye lacks "fixation points," increasing cognitive load. Using dot-leaders (`.....`) between a table of contents and page numbers creates an artificial bridge that reduces this strain.

> [!question] How is white space utilized as a tool?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> White space is the visual counter to what you see; gaps are used to separate items, aid in grouping and structure, and highlight important information.

### Real-World Critique: University Websites

> [!attention] Kelaniya vs. Cambridge Web Design
> 
> In a lecture critique, the proposed Kelaniya university website demonstrated severe flaws:
> 
> 1. Page Jitter: The layout jumps dynamically on load, rather than rendering smoothly, causing disorientation.
>     
> 2. Mismatched Grouping: Mixing physical places (Help Desk), Abstract concepts (Courses), and Tools into the same visual tier violates logical grouping.
>     
> 3. Overuse of All-Caps: Hurts readability.
>     
> 
> In contrast, the Cambridge University website utilizes excellent whitespace to create virtual boundaries. A prominent, high-quality background image anchors the eye, while sparse, clean text (not entirely bolded) provides an elegant, low-stress navigation path.

## 7. User Action and Affordances

> [!question] What are "affordances"?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Affordances are physical or psychological cues where an object's shape or size suggests how it should be used. For example, a mug handle affords grasping, and culturally, buttons afford pushing. On a screen, button-like objects afford clicking, establishing a culture of computer use.

> [!success] The Perfect Affordance: The Mug Handle
> 
> If a mug is filled with hot liquid, a proper handle allows you to interact with it safely.
> 
> - If placed too high: Hard to drink from.
>     
> - If placed too low: Center of gravity causes it to tilt, causing fatigue.
>     
> - Placed in the middle: Perfect affordance. Once society learns this design, all mugs universally follow it, despite differing colors/shapes. UI elements must follow established digital affordances (like raised borders for clickable buttons).
>     

## 8. Appropriate Appearance (Aesthetics & Culture)

> [!question] How do aesthetics and utility intersect in design?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Aesthetically pleasing designs increase user satisfaction and productivity. Sometimes beauty and utility conflict (e.g., using a visually pleasing background that makes text hard to read), but they can also work together as a key product differentiator, as seen in some consumer electronics. Interactivity can also soften strict design choices.

> [!question] What are the rules for using color and 3D effects?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Both are often used badly and should be used sparingly to reinforce information. Overuse without good reason is a mistake. Designers must account for color blindness, ensure good contrast, and verify readability by adjusting their monitor to greyscale. 3D is good for some physical information but fails in things like 3D pie charts or perspective text.

> [!warning] Color & Contrast
> 
> Do not rely solely on color to convey information. Always view your designs in greyscale to ensure contrast limits are respected.

### Globalization vs. Localization

> [!question] How do culture and globalization affect design?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> Localisation and internationalisation involve altering interfaces for specific languages or cultures (e.g., changing text sizes or left-to-right alignment). Globalisation attempts to use symbols that work everywhere. However, deeper cultural assumptions must be recognized, as symbols like a tick and a cross carry positive or negative meanings in some cultures, but both simply mean "mark this" in others.

> [!help] Cultural Context Examples
> 
> - White: Regarded as a color of peace in some cultures, the color of death/funerals in South Asian cultures, and treated entirely differently in Japan.
>     
> - Black: Often seen as dark or negative, but in Nordic countries, it is highly utilitarian (absorbing heat in the snow).
>     
> - Thumbs Up: Universally known as an "acceptance" button on social media, but in Brazil, it is considered a gross insult.
>     
> 
> Solution: Build configurable, theme-based architectures so your UI can localize based on cultural regions.

## 9. Iteration and Prototyping

Because we are designing for the unpredictable, irrational human mind, we rely heavily on iterative prototyping. You will never get it right on the first try.

> [!question] What are the main pitfalls of iteration and prototyping?
> 
> [!faq] Answer
> 
> While iteration (prototype, evaluate, redesign) is necessary because you "never get it right first time," a major pitfall is "moving little by little... but to where". Designers must ensure they have a good starting point and truly understand what is wrong with the design, rather than confusing a minor peak (the Malverns) for the ultimate goal (the Matterhorn).

> [!todo] Prototyping Strategy
> 
> Do not iterate aimlessly. Ensure your initial prototype is pointed in the correct general direction. It is easy to polish a terrible design and think you've succeeded just because the buttons are aligned, missing the fact that the workflow itself is fundamentally broken.

> [!done] Final Takeaway
> 
> Great interaction design happens when you stop thinking like an abstract programmer and start observing the messy, linear, and stressful realities of your concrete users.

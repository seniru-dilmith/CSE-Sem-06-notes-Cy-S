> [!abstract] Overview
> Understanding Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) requires a deep dive into the three core components: <span style="color: #3498db; font-weight: bold;">The Human</span>, <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">The Computer</span>, and <span style="color: #2ecc71; font-weight: bold;">The Interaction</span>. This note focuses on the human element, treating us as information processing systems influenced by biological limitations, cognitive structures, and emotional states.

## 1. Foundations of Human Information Processing

Humans deal with information in a systematic yet abstract manner. To design effective interfaces, we must understand how we receive, store, and process data.

> [!question] FAQ: What are the main ways humans interact with and process information?
> Humans interact through information input/output (visual, auditory, haptic, and movement), store information in memory (sensory, short-term, and long-term), and process and apply information (through reasoning, problem-solving, skill acquisition, and error-making). Furthermore, a person's capabilities are influenced by emotion and individual differences.

### Core Components of the Human "System":
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Information I/O:</span> Visual, auditory, haptic (touch), and movement.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Memory:</span> Sensory, short-term (working), and long-term memory.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Processing:</span> Reasoning, problem-solving, skill acquisition, and error detection.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Emotion:</span> Influences how capabilities are utilized or suppressed.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Individual Differences:</span> No two users are the same; we cannot simply categorize 8 billion people into rigid "baskets."

## 2. The Vision System

Vision is our primary input channel. It isn't just "seeing"; it is a two-stage process involving physics and cognitive interpretation.

> [!help] FAQ: How does the human vision system work?
> Vision occurs in two stages: the <span style="color: #9b59b6; font-weight: bold;">physical reception of the stimulus</span> and the <span style="color: #9b59b6; font-weight: bold;">processing and interpretation</span> of that stimulus. The eye receives light, which reflects off objects and is focused upside-down on the retina, and transforms it into electrical energy. The retina contains rods (for low light vision) and cones (for color vision), while ganglion cells detect patterns and movement.

### A. Physical Reception (The Eye)
- Light reflects from objects and enters through a small aperture.
- The <span style="font-weight: bold;">lens</span> focuses the image <span style="color: #e74c3c; font-weight: bold;">upside-down</span> on the retina.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Retina:</span> Contains <span style="font-weight: bold;">Rods</span> (sensitive to low light) and <span style="font-weight: bold;">Cones</span> (sensitive to color/detail).
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Ganglion Cells:</span> Brain-level neurons that detect patterns and movement.

> [!example] The Pinhole Effect & Brain Plasticity
> Just like a pinhole camera, the eye sees the world flipped. Interestingly, if you wore glasses that flipped your vision upside-down, your brain would eventually "re-flip" the image after a few days to make sense of the world. This shows that what we "see" is actually what our brain <span style="font-weight: bold;">interprets</span>.

### B. Interpreting the Signal
The brain uses several cues to make sense of the 2D image on the retina to understand a 3D world.

> [!info] FAQ: How does the brain interpret visual signals like size, depth, brightness, and color?
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Size and depth</span> are interpreted using the visual angle, visual acuity, familiar object sizes, and cues like overlapping.
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Brightness</span> is a subjective reaction to light levels (luminance).
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Color</span> is made up of hue, intensity, and saturation. Blue acuity is the lowest.

#### Visual Constraints:
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Visual Angle:</span> Indicates how much of the field of view an object occupies. A small object close up can have the same visual angle as a large object far away.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Visual Acuity:</span> Our limited ability to perceive fine detail.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Familiarity:</span> We perceive known objects (like a family member or our own car) as constant in size even if they are far away and technically appear tiny.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Color:</span> Cones are sensitive to wavelengths. 
    - <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">Gender Differences:</span> 8% of males are color blind compared to only 1% of females. Evolutionarily, females (gatherers) needed high color sensitivity to identify safe fruits/mushrooms, while males (hunters) optimized for movement detection.
    - <span style="color: #3498db; font-weight: bold;">Blue Acuity:</span> Humans have the lowest sensitivity to blue. This makes blue a "neutral" background color, but <span style="color: #e74c3c; font-weight: bold;">unsuitable for important fine details</span>.


> [!warning] Optical Illusions
> These occur due to <span style="font-weight: bold;">overcompensation</span>. The visual system tries to resolve ambiguity using context, sometimes resulting in a "wrong" interpretation (e.g., the Ponzo illusion makes the top bar look larger because of the converging "railway" lines).

## 3. Reading

Reading is a pattern-recognition task involving rapid eye movements.

> [!note] FAQ: What are the stages of reading?
> Reading involves perceiving a visual pattern, decoding it using an internal representation of language, and interpreting it using knowledge of syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. The physical act involves saccades and fixations. Word shape is crucial to recognition, and negative contrast improves reading from computer screens.

- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Saccades:</span> Rapid jumps from one point to another. No information is processed during the jump.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Fixations:</span> The pauses where the eye is stationary. This is where <span style="color: #27ae60; font-weight: bold;">perception occurs</span>.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Word Shape:</span> We don't read letter-by-letter as adults; we recognize the "envelope" or shape of the word. This is why you can often read jumbled sentences as long as the first and last letters are correct.

## 4. The Hearing System

Hearing provides environmental information about distance and direction.

> [!faq] FAQ: How does the human hearing system function?
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Physical apparatus:</span> Outer ear (protects/amplifies), Middle ear (transmits vibrations), Inner ear (chemical transmitters/nerve impulses).
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Sound characteristics:</span> Pitch (frequency), Loudness (amplitude), and Timbre (quality/nature of the sound).
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Range:</span> 20Hz to 15kHz.

- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Pitch:</span> Frequency of the sound.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Loudness:</span> Amplitude/energy.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Timbre:</span> The specific "color" of a sound (how you distinguish a flute from a piano, or one friend's voice from another).
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Cocktail Party Phenomenon:</span> The brain's ability to filter out background noise to focus on a single auditory stream (like a conversation in a noisy room).

## 5. Touch and Movement

### A. Touch (Haptic Perception)
Touch is a critical feedback loop, especially for the visually impaired.

> [!tip] FAQ: What is the role of touch in human perception?
> Stimuli are received via skin receptors: <span style="font-weight: bold;">thermoreceptors</span> (heat/cold), <span style="font-weight: bold;">nociceptors</span> (pain), and <span style="font-weight: bold;">mechanoreceptors</span> (pressure). Certain areas, like fingers and lips, are more sensitive due to higher neuron density.

- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Kinesthesis:</span> The "sixth sense"—awareness of your body's position. Skilled drivers often "superimpose" their vehicle's size onto their kinesthetic sense, feeling the car as an extension of their own body.

### B. Movement Control
Response time is a combination of <span style="color: #9b59b6; font-weight: bold;">Reaction Time</span> and <span style="color: #9b59b6; font-weight: bold;">Movement Time</span>.

> [!info] FAQ: How is human movement time calculated, and what is Fitts' Law?
> The time taken to respond is <span style="font-weight: bold;">reaction time + movement time</span>. Typical reaction times: Visual (~200ms), Auditory (~150ms), Pain (~700ms).
> <span style="font-weight: bold;">Fitts' Law:</span> $Mt = a + b \log_2(D/S + 1)$
> - $D$ = Distance to target
> - $S$ = Size of target
> - <span style="color: #27ae60; font-weight: bold;">Design Rule:</span> Make targets as large as possible and distances as small as possible.

## 6. Human Memory

Human memory isn't a single hard drive; it's a tiered system of buffers and storage.

> [!question] FAQ: What are the three types of human memory?
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Sensory Memory:</span> Continuously overwritten buffers (iconic/visual, echoic/aural, haptic/tactile).
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Short-term memory (STM):</span> A "scratch-pad" for temporary recall (rapid access ~70ms, rapid decay ~200ms). Capacity is $7 \pm 2$ chunks.
> - <span style="font-weight: bold;">Long-term memory (LTM):</span> Permanent repository (slow access ~100ms, unlimited capacity).

![[Pasted image 20260510012458.png]]
### Short-Term Memory (STM)

- Rapid Access ~70ms
- Rapid Decay ~200ms
- Limited Capacity 7 $\pm$ 2 chunks

The "RAM" of the brain. To overcome the $7 \pm 2$ limit, we use <span style="font-weight: bold;">Chunking</span>.
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Example:</span> It's hard to remember 10 random digits, but easy to remember a 10-digit phone number if it's broken into 3 chunks (e.g., 071-231-5788).

### Long-Term Memory (LTM)

- Slow Access ~0.1 seconds
- Slow Decay, if any
- huge or unlimited capacity

Divided into:
1. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Episodic Memory:</span> Serial memory of events/experiences (like a story).
2. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Semantic Memory:</span> Structured facts, concepts, and skills (derived from episodic memory).

#### Models of LTM:
- <span style="font-weight: bold;">Semantic Networks:</span> Nodes and links representing relationships and inheritance.

```mermaid
graph TD
    A[ANIMAL] -- breathes --> A
    A -- moves --> A
    B[DOG] -- is a --> A
    B -- barks --> B
    C[COLLIE] -- is a --> B
    C -- works sheep --> C
````

- Frames: Data structures with "slots" (Fixed, Default, Variable).
    
- Scripts: Stereotypical situations (e.g., "Visiting the Vet" involves entry conditions, roles, and scenes).
    
- Production Rules: IF-THEN procedural knowledge (e.g., IF dog growls THEN run away).
    

> [!important] The 21-Day Rule
> 
> Neurologically, it takes roughly 21 days of complete absence or practice to "reset" an index in the LTM or rewire a habit.

## 7. Thinking: Reasoning and Problem Solving

Thinking involves processing information to reach conclusions or solve tasks.

> [!note] FAQ: What are the different types of human reasoning?
> 
> - Deductive: Logically necessary conclusions (If A then B).
>     
> - Inductive: Generalizing from cases seen to cases unseen (Unreliable but useful).
>     
> - Abductive: Reasoning from event to cause (Highly unreliable).
>     

### Problem Solving Theories:

- Gestalt Theory: Solving through insight and restructuring.
    
- Problem Space Theory: Moving through "states" using legal operators (like a maze).
    
- Analogy: Mapping knowledge from one domain to a new one.
    
- Skill Acquisition: Moving from conscious processing to "chunked" automatic behavior.
    

---

## 8. Errors: Slips vs. Mistakes

> [!danger] FAQ: What is the difference between a slip and a mistake?
> 
> - Slips: Right intention, wrong execution (e.g., meaning to press 'Off' but accidentally hitting 'On'). Caused by poor skill or inattention.
>     
> - Mistakes: Wrong intention (e.g., believing the 'Off' button actually performs a different task). Caused by incorrect mental models.
>

## 9. Emotion and Interface Design

Emotion is not just a "feeling"; it is a biological response called Affect.

> [!success] FAQ: How does emotion influence human capabilities and interface design?
> 
> - Positive Affect: Leads to creative problem solving and makes difficult tasks feel easier.
>     
> - Negative Affect: Narrows thinking and makes even easy tasks feel hard.
>     
> - Design Impact: Relaxed users are more forgiving of design flaws. Aesthetically pleasing interfaces increase positive affect.
>

## 10. Individual Differences

Designers must consider:

- Long-term: Gender, physical/intellectual abilities.
    
- Short-term: Stress, fatigue.
    
- Changing: Age.
    

> [!caution] Final Check
> 
> Always ask: "Will this design decision exclude a section of the user population?" Good design is Universal Design.

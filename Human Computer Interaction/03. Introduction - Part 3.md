[[01. Introduction - Part 1]] [[02. Introduction - Part 2]]
> [!abstract] summary
> <span style="color: #4a90e2; font-weight: bold;">Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)</span> is a highly interdisciplinary field heavily grounded in Computer Science. It draws heavily from psychology, anthropology, and design to understand human behavior and create effective, usable systems. This comprehensive note covers the chronological milestones of HCI, its foundational theories, experimental paradigms, qualitative methods, and profound real-world impacts explored during the lecture, with deep dives into the lecturer's specific anecdotes and industry observations.

> [!tldr] 
> <span style="font-weight: bold;">Key Takeaway:</span> Technology almost always outpaces societal readiness. Concepts from the 1960s and 1980s (like GUIs, touch screens, and multi-touch) took decades to become commercially viable. Successful HCI requires mapping accurate mental, cultural, and environmental models to technological solutions while navigating the strict tradeoff between internal and external validity.

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">1. Historical Milestones of HCI and Market Readiness</span>

The evolution of HCI is not simply a timeline of scientific inventions, but a timeline of when <span style="font-style: italic; color: #8e44ad;">society was actually ready to adopt them</span>. The lecture heavily emphasized that researchers are often well ahead of their time, and innovations only become commercial successes when the market has the purchasing power and the fundamental need for them.

## <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">The 1960s: The Genesis of Modern Computing</span>
The 1960s laid the foundational dreams of HCI. 
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Ivan Sutherland</span> invented computer graphics and the first graphical user interface (GUI) with his system, <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Sketchpad</span>. This was his PhD thesis at MIT in 1963. He utilized a stylus and a touch-sensitive screen - concepts that were radically ahead of their time. Every company saw this idea, but they had to wait decades until the masses were ready to adopt it.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Douglas Engelbart</span> invented the very first computer mouse.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Alan Kay</span>, who later received the Turing Award in 2003 for object-oriented programming (SmallTalk), demonstrated Sutherland's Sketchpad. He also created the GRAphical Input Language (GRAIL) in 1969 and conceptualized the Dynabook in 1968.

> [!quote] The Gap Between Research and Reality
> <span style="font-style: italic; color: #7f8c8d;">"Sometimes we are well ahead of the society needs. And we just abandoned it maybe after two or three years. While after few years down the line, you may see a similar product with maybe half of the features that you originally thought of. So understand that gap."</span> 

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What were the major HCI breakthroughs in the 1960s?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: In the 1960s, Ivan Sutherland invented computer graphics and the first graphical user interface (GUI) with his system called Sketchpad, which was also his PhD thesis at MIT in 1963. During the same decade, Douglas Engelbart invented the first computer mouse.</span>
>
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: Who is Alan Kay and what were his contributions?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: Alan Kay is a pioneer who demonstrated Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad, the GRAphical Input Language (GRAIL) from 1969, and his own Dynabook concept from 1968. He received the Turing Award in 2003 for his contributions to object-oriented programming, specifically SmallTalk.</span>

## <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">Late 1970s to 1980s: The Paradigm Shift</span>
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">IBM</span> created the Personal Computer (PC).
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Xerox PARC</span> invented the <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">WIMP</span> paradigm (<span style="font-weight: bold;">W</span>indows, <span style="font-weight: bold;">I</span>cons, <span style="font-weight: bold;">M</span>enu, <span style="font-weight: bold;">P</span>ointer) in the late 1970s. They demonstrated the Star workstation in 1985.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Apple</span> released the Macintosh computer in 1984. This was the <span style="text-decoration: underline;">first commercial computer</span> to utilize a GUI over a Command Line Interface (CLI).
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Mark Weiser</span> coined the term <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"Ubiquitous computing"</span> in 1988, though it took another 20 years for it to become a reality.

> [!example] The Multi-Touch Reality Check (CHI 1985)
> A defining example of technology outpacing adoption is the <span style="font-weight: bold;">CHI 1985</span> paper: <span style="font-style: italic;">"A Multi-Touch Three Dimensional Touch-Sensitive Tablet"</span>. 
> ![[Pasted image 20260403203020.png]]
> This prototype tablet could sense multiple points of contact simultaneously, measure the degree of contact independently, and utilized recursive area subdivision algorithms and interpolation schemes to maintain high resolution across discrete grid points. 
> 
> <span style="color: #e74c3c; font-weight: bold;">Lecturer's Insight:</span> If you presented this paper title today without the 1985 date, students would think it is a brand new final year project. There might even be groups inquiring to build it. The mathematics, the image processing, and the algorithms were mapped out 36 years ago. What was lacking was market readiness.

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: How did personal computers and graphical interfaces evolve in the late 1970s and 1980s?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: IBM created the Personal Computer (PC), and Xerox PARC invented the WIMP paradigm. In 1984, Apple released the Macintosh, the first commercial computer to feature a GUI instead of a CLI. A demonstration of the Xerox PARC Star workstation further showcased these advancements in 1985.</span>
> ![[Pasted image 20260403203154.png]]
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: Are multi-touch interfaces a recent invention?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: No, most "new" UI technology is not actually that new. For example, a multi-touch, three-dimensional touch-sensitive tablet was already being presented in a paper at the CHI 1985 conference.</span>

## <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">1990s to Mid 2000s: Commoditization</span>
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Early-Mid 90s:</span> GUIs became a widespread commodity. Manufacturers commercialized handheld devices like the Apple Newton and Palm.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Late 90s-Mid 2000s:</span> Mobile phones became capable of running GUIs. Microsoft released the Tablet PC, and Blackberry carved a niche for email-focused phones featuring physical mini-QWERTY keyboards.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">2006:</span> Jeff Han demonstrated a cheap method for creating large-display touch-screens at UIST 2006, making global headlines.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">2007:</span> Apple released the iPhone, substantially elevating user expectations for mobile devices and making touch-screens an absolute commodity.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">2011:</span> Microsoft released the Kinect.

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">2. The Present and The Research Frontier</span>

> [!info] 
> The technological dreams of the 1960s are reality today. We possess advanced touch-screen mobile devices and computers that dwarf the supercomputers of a decade ago. We moved from compiling LaTeX documents in the dark to true WYSIWYG (What You See Is What You Get) document editors.

<span style="font-weight: bold; color: #16a085;">Current Realities:</span>
* High-accuracy continuous speech recognition.
* Machine translation bridging numerous languages.
* Augmented reality systems, such as real-time menu translation on smartphones.

> [!tip] hint
> According to the transcript, <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">"The dreams of today will become a reality in maybe another 15 to 20 years time."</span> The engineering and mathematics for things like hyperloops, 100-year nuclear batteries (nickel/cobalt ones that are currently too expensive for the market), and foldable organic screens are already mapped out. The delay is primarily market viability, mass purchasing power, and safety regulations.

<span style="font-weight: bold; color: #16a085;">The Contemporary Research Frontier:</span>
* Pervasive and ubiquitous computing.
* Tangible computing.
* Surface computing.
* Interaction with massive, wall-sized displays.
* Intelligent interactive systems.
* Crowdsourcing and human computational systems.
* Developing highly refined theories for modeling and understanding human behavior.

> [!example] Extreme HCI Futures (Transcript Deep-Dive)
> The lecture elaborated on incredible frontiers that blur the line between biology and machinery:
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Brain-Computer Interfaces:</span> Voice replaced typing, and soon, brain waves will replace voice. Technology already exists to vaguely recreate human thought patterns via algorithms sensing brain activity.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Quantum Computing:</span> Shifting from binary states to multi-state computing, vastly expanding the search space for complex optimization problems.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Cyborgs & Medical HCI:</span> Replacing organs with advanced cybernetic materials. Imagine an artificial heart with a tiny communication device that feeds data to a remote hospital server daily. Before the patient even feels the symptoms of a heart attack, medical intervention is already on the way.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Nanotechnology & Phages:</span> Using specialized viruses (phages) modified to attack antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Administering these phages effectively requires intense computing, optimization algorithms, and a new form of HCI happening *inside* the human body.

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What "dreams of the 1960s" have become a reality today?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: Today, we have advanced touch-screen mobile devices and computers faster than decade-old supercomputers. Realized dreams include high-accuracy continuous speech recognition, cross-language machine translation, and augmented reality systems.</span>

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">3. The Three Pillars of HCI and Human Anthropology</span>

HCI is fundamentally a blend of three primary research activities:
1. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Creating usability methods for guiding design</span>: Developing heuristics like the "Eight golden rules", following strict medical guidelines for bodily HCI, and capturing the abstract metric of "user experience".
2. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Creating new systems, tools, and techniques</span>.
3. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Modelling and understanding human behaviour</span>.

> [!note] The Evolution of Tools (Transcript Context)
> The creation of new tools is the crux of human advancement. The lecturer noted that human evolution into a "super race" is largely owed to the human thumb, which provided an enormously higher degree of physical freedom. The thumb became our <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">first tool</span>, allowing early humans to grasp stones, strike them to create fire, and build primitive hammers. This initiated a recursive cycle where primary tools are used to build more complex tools (from stone tools to computers to future technologies). HCI sits at the apex of this continuous tool-building lineage.

## <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">The Complexity of the Human User</span>
Modelling human behavior is the most volatile and difficult branch of HCI. Why? Because we aren't dealing with unconscious variables; we are dealing with conscious, emotional, and often irrational beings.

> [!warning] caution attention
> <span style="font-weight: bold;">The Excuse vs. The Root Cause:</span> Humans have a remarkable way of inventing rational-sounding "reasons" to mask their actual underlying emotional triggers. A user might smash a keyboard or throw their phone away and claim "the text input is too slow", when in reality, the root cause is external stress. The phone they threw away is still hundreds of times faster than the technology from five years ago, yet they are unhappy. HCI researchers must parse out the true root causes from the surface-level excuses.

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What are the three primary research activities that make up HCI?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: 1. Creating usability methods to guide design. 2. Creating new systems, tools, and techniques. 3. Modelling and understanding human behavior.</span>

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">4. Human Psychology, Culture, and Environmental Factors</span>

The lecture provided vivid, real-world examples of how environment and culture manipulate HCI interactions. A user's background completely changes how they interact with a system.

> [!example] Real-World Behavioral Modeling
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Cultural Frustration Tolerance:</span> In developing nations with limited resources and intense competition, users often exist in a state of high stress. Consequently, they have zero tolerance for system errors, reacting with intense frustration (e.g., furious honking in traffic for a slight delay). In contrast, users in highly developed, stable societies might be more forgiving of a system glitch and try to understand it from the designer's point of view.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">The COVID-19 Adaptation:</span> When the first COVID-19 patient was identified in Sri Lanka, the societal model broke down into absolute panic - neighbors even plotted to burn the patient's house down. As the virus spread to thousands, the societal model adapted, and the disease became normalized. Designing a system for a society in "Panic Mode" versus "Adapted Mode" requires entirely different psychological frameworks.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Social Media Conditioning:</span> Over the last 10 years, platforms like Facebook have fundamentally re-engineered human values. By creating highly accessible publishing platforms, they exploit human psychology (status anxiety, jealousy over vacation photos) and flood networks with vast amounts of content, ranging from literature to nasty gossip and petitions. The impact of this technology depends entirely on how user psychology is modeled.
> * <span style="font-weight: bold;">Group Dynamics:</span> The way a user behaves in a group of university students is different from a group of high schoolers. Furthermore, after a few years in the corporate sector, that same individual will be disciplined into a completely different, more appropriate behavioral model.

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">5. Experimental Psychology & The Validity Tradeoff</span>

Because humans are so complex, HCI borrows heavily from <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #9b59b6;">Experimental Psychology</span>, which provides models of our perceptual abilities and neuromuscular motor control.

The cornerstone of this is the <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">Controlled Experiment Paradigm</span>, which demands:
* A set of independent and dependent variables.
* A formulation of a set of hypotheses.
* A method description that outlines a highly reproducible experimental procedure.
* Analysis of results, often involving statistical hypothesis tests.

## <span style="color: #2980b9; font-weight: bold;">The Critical Problem: Model Integrity</span>
An experiment inherently assumes a <span style="font-weight: bold;">model</span>. If the model designed in the experiment does not map accurately to the chaotic reality of actual users, the results are completely worthless. 

> [!danger] error
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">When Models Fail (The SLSI Example):</span>
> The lecturer brought up the recent coconut oil issue in Sri Lanka. The assumed model is that the Sri Lanka Standards Institution (SLSI) looks after taxpayer health and safety. However, if a person appointed has a conflict of interest regarding dangerous chemical imports, the entire model loses its internal and external validity. A release note is issued, the system believes the certification, but the truth is compromised because the foundational model was wrong.

> [!danger] error
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">The Prince Charming Caveat:</span>
> When creating an experimental model, you must explicitly state the caveats and limitations (the terms of use). If you design an interface based on a model where the user is perfectly calm and attentive (like a "Prince Charming"), that system will instantly fail when the user interacts with it while angry, distracted, or in a rush. If the conditions do not match, the model cannot be applied.

This leads directly to the core tradeoff between:
* <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #2980b9;">Internal Validity:</span> How tightly controlled the experiment is (e.g., controlling oxygen and carbon dioxide levels perfectly in a 9th-grade science lab).
* <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #27ae60;">External Validity:</span> How well the findings generalize to the real, unpredictable world.

> [!failure] fail missing
> <span style="font-weight: bold;">Ecological Validity:</span> If an experiment is controlled so heavily to ensure internal validity that it completely damages its real-world implications, the results do not generalize. This means the experimental model failed to capture the finer nuances of everyday life, resulting in an <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"unacceptably low ecological validity"</span>.

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: How has experimental psychology influenced HCI?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: It provides models of the human neuromuscular motor control system and perceptual abilities. It heavily influences HCI through the "controlled experiment paradigm," which involves setting variables, hypotheses, reproducible procedures, and statistical tests.</span>
>
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What is the tradeoff between internal and external validity in experiments?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: Experimental designs assume a model. There is an inherent tradeoff where ensuring high internal validity (strict control) can make the model too unrealistic, thereby severely damaging the external validity (how well the findings generalize to the real world).</span>
>
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What does it mean if an experiment has "low ecological validity"?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: It means the experiment's results do not generalize to actual, real-world use practices because the experimental model fails to capture the finer nuances of everyday life.</span>

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">6. Moving Beyond Controlled Experiments</span>

Because perfect controlled experiments are rarely possible with living, breathing humans without sacrificing ecological validity, HCI researchers rely on diverse qualitative and ethnographic methods:
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Ethnographic research:</span> Used to study actual practices of use in the wild.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Anecdotal experiences:</span> Recording and analyzing subjective user reactions.
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Qualitative Data Protocols:</span> 
  * <span style="color: #2980b9;">Think - aloud protocols</span> 
  * <span style="color: #2980b9;">Structured interviews</span> 
  * <span style="color: #2980b9;">Video coding and transcription</span> 
* <span style="font-weight: bold;">Alternative Approaches:</span> Surveys, opinion polls, focus groups, participatory design, case studies, and the analysis of automated server logs.

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: Since controlled experiments have limitations, what other methods do HCI researchers use?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: Researchers utilize ethnographic methods to study actual practices of use, and record subjective reactions. They collect qualitative data using think-aloud protocols, structured interviews, and video coding. Other approaches include surveys, focus groups, participatory design, and analyzing automated logs.</span>

# <span style="color: #e67e22; font-weight: bold;">7. Summary & Field Outlook</span>

> [!success] check done
> <span style="font-weight: bold;">HCI is massively interdisciplinary</span>. It incorporates psychological behavior theories, neuropsychological motor control, social science, anthropology, and design.

However, at its core, <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b;">HCI is grounded in Computer Science</span>. It is an unavoidable, core pillar of modern CS and Engineering curriculums worldwide. Just as calculus is grounded in mathematics, HCI is grounded in computer science. What practitioners do directly dictates how computer systems are architected and built. It is impossible to be a high-quality software engineer today without being deeply literate in HCI. 

> [!bug] The Rapidly Transitional Nature of HCI
> Unlike subjects like constraint programming or pure mathematics which can remain static for decades while researchers work on long-lasting problems, HCI is currently in an aggressive, rapid transitional period. As noted in the transcript, if you attend top tier-one conferences like CHI or UIST, the hot topics from just two or three years ago are completely different from what is happening today. The momentum shifts incredibly fast, blurring the lines between academic research (identifying what to build) and industrial production (actually delivering the solutions to users).

> [!question] help FAQ
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: What academic theories base the field of HCI?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: HCI research relies heavily on interdisciplinary fields: psychological theories of human behavior, neuropsychological theories of motor control and perception, as well as theories from social science, anthropology, and design.</span>
>
> <span style="font-weight: bold; color: #d35400;">Q: Why is HCI ultimately grounded in Computer Science?</span>
> <span style="color: #27ae60;">A: Despite its interdisciplinary nature, HCI directly influences how new computer systems are built. The field involves building new systems and inventing interaction techniques. Therefore, computer scientists must be literate in HCI to deliver high-quality, usable systems.</span>

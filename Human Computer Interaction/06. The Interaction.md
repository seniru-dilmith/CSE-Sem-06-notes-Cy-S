## 1. What is Interaction?

At its core, <b style="color: #3498db;">interaction</b> is the communication between the <b style="color: #e67e22;">user</b> and the <b style="color: #e67e22;">system</b>. However, as noted in the lecture, it is much more than just **duplex** communication—it is about <b style="color: #2ecc71;">understanding</b>. 

> [!quote] 
> "If your interaction is fairly successful, users should not feel that they are interacting with something artificial... it's simply an <span style="color: #9b59b6;">extended dupe</span> (extension) of your own values and traits."

> [!help] FAQ: What are the main components of human-computer interaction?
> <b style="color: #3498db;">Interaction</b> involves:
> * <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Translations</b> between the user and the system.
> * <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Ergonomics</b>: The physical characteristics of interaction.
> * <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Interaction Styles</b>: The nature of the user/system dialog.
> * <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Context</b>: Social, organizational, and motivational factors.

---

## 2. Terms of Interaction

To analyze interaction, we must define the framework in which it happens:

* <b style="color: #e74c3c;">Domain</b>: The area of work under study (e.g., Graphic Design, Banking). The domain defines the <span style="color: #95a5a6;">abstract level framework</span> in your mind. You act differently in a banking domain (serious/careful) compared to a gaming domain (casual).
* <b style="color: #e74c3c;">Goal</b>: What you want to achieve (e.g., "Create a solid red triangle" or "Get a printout").
* <b style="color: #e74c3c;">Task</b>: How you go about doing it. This is realized through operations or actions on the interface.

> [!important] 
> The <b style="color: #3498db;">Task-Goal</b> distinction is crucial. The goal is the <span style="color: #2ecc71;">intent</span>, while the task is the <span style="color: #e67e22;">execution</span> provided by the interface design.

## 3. Donald Norman’s Model

Donald Norman proposed a <b style="color: #3498db;">Seven Stage Model</b> of interaction, concentrating on the <b style="color: #e67e22;">user's view</b> of the interface.

### The Seven Stages
1. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Establish the Goal</b> (e.g., I need a physical copy of this file).
2. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Formulate Intention</b> (Decide to print it, double-sided).
3. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Specify Actions</b> at the interface (Go to File > Print).
4. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Execute Action</b> (Click the button).
5. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Perceive System State</b> (Look for a progress bar or printer noise).
6. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Interpret System State</b> (Does the lack of noise mean it's finished or crashed?).
7. <b style="color: #f1c40f;">Evaluate System State</b> with respect to the goal (Do I have my paper?).

> [!help] FAQ: How does Donald Norman’s model describe the interaction process?
> 
> Norman's model focuses on the user's view and describes a seven-stage execution/evaluation loop. The user establishes a goal, formulates an intention, specifies and executes actions, and then perceives, interprets, and evaluates the resulting system state against the original goal.

## 4. The Gulfs of Interaction

Why are some systems hard to use? Norman explains this through two "Gulfs."

- Gulf of Execution: The difference between the user's formulation of actions and the actions allowed by the system. If you want to do $X$ but the system only allows $Y$, you are in this gulf.
    
- Gulf of Evaluation: The difference between the system's actual state and the user's expectation/perception of that state. If a system does something but gives no feedback, the user cannot bridge this gulf.
    

> [!help] FAQ: What are the "Gulf of Execution" and the "Gulf of Evaluation"?
> 
> - Gulf of Execution: Occurs when user-formulated actions do not match system-allowed actions.
>     
> - Gulf of Evaluation: Occurs when the user’s expectation of the changed state doesn't match the actual presentation.
>

## 5. Human Error: Slips and Mistakes

Interaction models help us categorize errors:

1. Slips:
    
    - The user understands the system and has the correct goal.
        
    - The action formulation is correct, but the execution is incorrect (e.g., clicking the wrong button due to poor focus).
        
    - Fix: Better interface design, safeguards, and confirmation dialogs.
        
2. Mistakes:
    
    - The user does not understand the system well.
        
    - They may not even have the right goal (e.g., using MS Word to make a presentation for a large audience).
        
    - Fix: Better user manuals, training, and FAQs.
        

> [!help] FAQ: How do slips differ from mistakes in human error?
> 
> A slip happens when the goal is right but the action is wrong (fix with design). A mistake happens when the goal itself is wrong because the user doesn't understand the system (fix with training).

## 6. Abowd and Beale Framework

This is an extension of Norman’s model, viewing interaction as a translation between languages.

![[Pasted image 20260511200031.png]]

- There are 4 parts: User (U), Input (I), System (S), and Output (O).
    
- Each part has its own "language."
    
- Interaction = Translation between these languages.
    
- Problems = Failures in translation.
    

> [!help] FAQ: What is the Abowd and Beale interaction framework?
> 
> It is an extension of Norman's model that divides interaction into User, Input, System, and Output. It posits that since each component has a unique language, interaction is essentially translation; thus, interaction failures are translation problems.

## 7. Ergonomics (Human Factors)

Ergonomics is the study of the physical characteristics of interaction. It defines standards for:

- Arrangement of Controls: Grouping by function, frequency of use, or sequence. 
    
- Environment: Adaptable seating (to fit different sizes) and lighting.
    
- Health Issues: Managing noise, temperature, humidity, and posture.
    
- Color: Standard usage (Red = Warning, Green = OK) and awareness of color-blindness.
    

> [!help] FAQ: What is ergonomics and what factors does it consider?
> 
> Ergonomics (human factors) is the study of physical interaction traits. It considers the arrangement of controls (grouped by function), environment (seating), health (lighting/noise), and color usage.

## 8. Office vs. Industrial Interfaces

The context of the environment changes the interaction design requirements significantly:

|**Feature**|**Office Interface**|**Industrial Interface**|
|---|---|---|
|Data Type|Textual|Numeric|
|Change Rate|Slow|Fast (Heat, Pressure)|
|Environment|Clean / Air-conditioned|Dirty / Noisy / Oil|
|Manipulation|Direct (Artificial World)|Indirect (Real World via Instruments)|

> [!caution] The "Oil Soaked Mouse"
> 
> In industrial settings, mechanical components like mice can fail because oil/dirt from the user's hands accumulates inside. This is a physical constraint of interaction.

> [!help] FAQ: How do office interfaces differ from industrial interfaces?
> 
> Office interfaces involve slow-changing textual data in clean areas using direct manipulation. Industrial interfaces involve fast numeric data in dirty areas using indirect manipulation through instruments, often with feedback delays.

### Glass Interfaces

In industry, traditional dials/knobs are being replaced by Glass Interfaces (screens and keypads).

- Pros: Cheaper, flexible, multiple representations, precise values.
    
- Cons: No physical location context, can be too complex, loss of "feel."
    

> [!help] FAQ: What are "glass interfaces" in industrial settings?
> 
> They replace physical knobs with screens and keypads. While flexible and precise, they lose the physical context of the controls and can lead to complex interfaces.

## 9. Interaction Styles

Interaction is a dialogue between computer and user. Common styles include:

1. Command Line Interface (CLI): Direct access to functionality. Good for experts and repetitive tasks (e.g., Unix).
    
2. Menus: Options are visible, reducing recall and favoring recognition.
    
3. Natural Language: Familiar but vague/ambiguous. Systems usually only understand a subset.
    
4. Form-fills: Primarily for data entry; screen looks like a paper form.
    
5. Spreadsheets: Sophisticated variation of form-filling using grids and formulas.
    
6. WIMP: Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers. The standard for PCs.
    
7. 3D Interfaces: Use light, occlusion, and distance to create virtual space.
    

> [!help] FAQ: What are the common interaction styles?
> 
> Styles include CLI, Menus, Natural Language, Query Dialogues, Form-fills, Spreadsheets, WIMP, Point and Click, and 3D interfaces.

> [!help] FAQ: What are the advantages and disadvantages of command line and natural language interfaces?
> 
> CLI offers direct access and is efficient for experts but hard for novices. Natural Language is familiar but prone to vagueness and ambiguity, making it technically difficult to implement perfectly.

## 10. Elements of the WIMP Interface

> [!info]
> 
> WIMP stands for Windows, Icons, Menus, and Pointers.

- Windows: Independent areas. Can overlap or be "tiled." Feature scrollbars and title bars.
    
- Icons: Small pictures representing objects/actions. Can be "iconised" (closed down).
    
- Pointers: Use mice, trackpads, or joysticks to select.
    
- Menus:
    
    - Pull-down: Click to see.
        
    - Fall-down: Hover to see.
        
    - Contextual: Right-click pop-ups.
        
    - Pie Menus: Circular, targets are equidistant (good for games/Fitts' Law).
        
- Buttons:
    
    - Radio buttons: Mutually exclusive (Pick one).
        
    - Check boxes: Non-exclusive (Pick many).
        
- Dialogue Boxes: Pop-ups for important events or information requests (e.g., Save File).
    

> [!help] FAQ: What is a WIMP interface and what are its elements?
> 
> WIMP is Windows, Icons, Menus, and Pointers. It also includes buttons (radio/checkbox), toolbars, palettes (tear-off menus), and dialogue boxes.

## 11. Interactivity: Look and Feel

- Look: Appearance (WIMP elements).
    
- Feel: Behavior (How the system responds to dragging, clicking, etc.).
    
- Look + Behavior = Look and Feel. (e.g., MacOS vs Windows menus).
    

> [!important] Initiative
> 
> - Computer-led: Old question-answer systems.
>     
> - User-led: Modern WIMP systems.
>     
> - Exceptions: Modal Dialogue Boxes are pre-emptive—they won't go away until you respond. Use them sparingly!
>     

> [!help] FAQ: How should systems handle interaction errors?
> 
> Since errors are unavoidable, the system should make them easy to detect so users can repair them. Speech-driven interfaces, for example, should reflect back their understanding for user confirmation.

## 12. Context: Social, Organizational, and Psychological

Interaction doesn't happen in a vacuum. It is affected by:

- Other People: Desire to impress, competition, or fear of failure.
    
- Motivation: Ambition, allegiance, or self-satisfaction.
    
- Psychology of Experience:
    
    - Flow: The balance between anxiety (too hard) and boredom (too easy).
        
    - Zone of Proximal Development: Things you can do with a little bit of help.
        

### The Cracker Analogy (Experience Design)

The lecture compares Real Crackers (cheap, paper hat, surprise bang) with Virtual Crackers.

- To make a virtual experience work, the system must build suspense (slow page opening) and surprise (WAV file "bang").
    

> [!help] FAQ: How does context impact human-computer interaction?
> 
> Interaction is influenced by social/organizational context (competition, fear of failure) and motivation. Inadequate systems cause frustration, leading to a lack of motivation.

## 13. Managing Value

People use a system ONLY IF its Perceived Value > Cost (Money, Time, Learning Effort).

### Discounted Future

In economics, we discount future value. In HCI, users do the same. They resist learning because the cost (effort now) feels higher than the value (time saved later).

- Rule: Interfaces need low barriers to entry and high perceived present value.
    

> [!help] FAQ: What makes a good user experience and how is value measured?
> 
> A good experience makes people want to use the system (e.g., achieving "flow"). Value is measured by whether perceived benefit exceeds cost (learning effort, download time).

## 14. Physical Design and Fluidity

Design is about Trade-offs.

- Example: Cooker controls on the front panel are safer for adults (easy to reach) but dangerous for children (easy to touch).
    
- Example: Minidisk remote controls need to be big (ergonomics) but have no room (physical). Solution: Multifunction "spring-back" controls.

> [!tip] Fluidity
> 
> Do external physical aspects reflect logical effects?
> 
> - Inverse actions: Twisting a knob left/right should have inverse logical effects.
>     
> - Compliant Interaction: The mechanical state (button pressed in) should reveal the internal logical state (On).
>

> > [!help] What is physical "fluidity" and "compliant interaction"?
> >
> > Fluidity means physical actions reflect logical effects (e.g., inverse actions). Compliant interaction means a control's physical state (like a mechanical button) clearly shows its logical state.

## 15. Organizational Design Strategies

If you want someone to do something, you have three options:

1. Coercion: "Do it or you lose your job." (Value = survival).
    
2. Enculturation: Explain corporate values and establish support (e.g., share options).
    
3. Emergence: Design the process so that individual values automatically align with organizational goals.
    

> [!done] Final Lesson
> 
> If you want someone to do something:
> 
> 1. Make it easy for them!
>     
> 2. Understand their values.
>     

> [!help] FAQ: How can organizations design value into a system?
> 
> Through coercion (mandates), enculturation (corporate values), or emergence (aligning individual and org value). The key is understanding user values and making tasks easy.
